Selling

Writing a listing that doesn't waste anyone's time

Most private listings are written to attract everyone, which is why they attract the wrong people. A good listing does the opposite job: it disqualifies fast, so the person who shows up is the person who already decided to buy it.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

Seven rewrites below. The "as written" lines are composites — phrasings we see constantly, assembled for this piece rather than quoted from any real listing. The rewrites are what we would put in their place, and the reasoning matters more than the exact words, because your car is not the car in the example.

As written
MUST SELL!!! Great car, runs and drives excellent. No lowballers, I know what I have.
Rewritten
2018 sedan, 84,300 miles, one owner since 2019, clean New Jersey title in hand. Selling because we've replaced it and I don't need three cars in the driveway.
Why

"Must sell" advertises that you are under pressure, which is an invitation to wait you out. "No lowballers" advertises that you expect to be insulted, which primes the conversation to be adversarial before it starts. And "I know what I have" is, in practice, the sentence people write when the price is high.

The rewrite trades all of that for facts a buyer can check and a reason for selling that is boring. Boring is the goal. A mundane, specific reason for selling removes the largest unspoken question in any private sale.

As written
Always well maintained. Never had any problems.
Rewritten
Oil and filter every 5,000 miles, receipts from 2021 onward in a folder. Front brakes and rotors done last spring, battery replaced in January. Timing service has not been done — I'd have it checked against the manufacturer's schedule for this engine.
Why

"Well maintained" is the single most common phrase in used car listings and carries no information whatsoever, because nobody has ever described their own car as poorly maintained. It is a word that costs you credibility rather than earning it.

Specific jobs with dates are checkable. Naming the thing that has not been done is the move most sellers flinch at and it is the one that buys you the most trust — it tells the reader that the rest of the list is complete rather than curated.

As written
Minor cosmetic damage, nothing major.
Rewritten
Door ding on the driver's rear quarter, about the size of a quarter (photo 11). Scuff on the rear bumper corner from a post (photo 12). Front driver's wheel is curbed. Windshield has a chip low on the passenger side, not in the sweep.
Why

"Minor" is your opinion of a thing the buyer has not seen, and the gap between your "minor" and theirs is where a deal dies in a driveway. Every flaw you name and photograph is one that cannot be re-discovered on arrival and used to reopen a settled price.

This is the paragraph that makes the whole listing credible. A buyer who reads four honest flaws stops hunting for a fifth.

As written
Needs nothing, just drive it home.
Rewritten
What it will need soon: rear tires are at about half tread, and the rear brakes were measured as getting thin at the last service. Nothing is unsafe now; both are jobs I'd plan for within the year.
Why

No used car needs nothing. Everyone reading knows that, so the claim reads as either careless or untrue, and it makes a buyer assume you have not looked. Pre-stating the near-term costs also takes them off the negotiating table — they are already in the price you set, and you can say so.

As written
$8,500 OBO. Make me an offer.
Rewritten
$8,500. I priced it against comparable cars in this area with similar mileage and took the tires into account. There's a little room for someone who's ready to go, not a lot.
Why

"OBO" and "make me an offer" hand the first number to the buyer, and the buyer's first number is always designed to be rejected. You will then spend three days negotiating up from a figure you never would have proposed.

Stating how you arrived at the price does two things: it signals you will not be moved by theater, and it gives a reasonable buyer the vocabulary to make a reasonable offer. We make the same argument about posting the price on our own windshields.

As written
Serious inquiries only. No tire kickers, no scammers, no trades, don't ask if it's available, it's available.
Rewritten
Happy to arrange a pre-purchase inspection at a shop of your choosing — I'd want the same. Available weekday evenings and Saturday mornings; text is easiest.
Why

A wall of prohibitions does not deter a single time-waster; it only tells a serious buyer what kind of conversation to expect. Replacing it with an inspection offer filters far more effectively — the people who are not serious do not take you up on it, and the people who are relax immediately.

As written
Clean title, runs and drives, $8,500, text 555-0100.
Rewritten
Clean title in my name, in hand — no loan to pay off, so we can sign and you can register it the same day. Both keys included. Text is easiest and I'll answer with the VIN so you can run your own history report.
Why

"Clean title" and "title in hand, no lien" are different claims, and the second is worth real money because it removes the awkward waiting period that a payoff creates. If you do still owe on the car, say so and describe the process — it's perfectly workable, and a buyer who learns about it late feels ambushed.

Offering the VIN unprompted is the cheapest credibility in the listing. It also saves you the message exchange where someone asks for it and you wonder whether you should send it.

The order a listing should run in

Most people write listings as a paragraph of feelings followed by a price. Reverse the priority: the information a buyer needs to disqualify the car should be at the top, because a fast no costs you nothing and a slow no costs you a Saturday.

  1. Year, model, trim, mileage, transmission, drivetrain The six facts that decide whether to keep reading.
  2. Title status and whether you hold it Clean, in your name, in hand — or the payoff situation, stated plainly.
  3. Price, and how you set it One number. Not a range, not OBO.
  4. Maintenance, with dates What's been done, what hasn't, what records exist.
  5. Every flaw, itemized and photo-numbered The paragraph that makes everything above believable.
  6. What it will need soon Tires, brakes, an upcoming service. Already priced in.
  7. Why you're selling Short, specific, unremarkable.
  8. How to reach you and when you're available Plus the inspection offer. This is your filter.

The test for any sentence in your listing. Could the owner of a car with a serious hidden problem write this exact sentence? If yes — "runs great," "well maintained," "must sell," "needs nothing" — it is doing no work for you, because the reader has learned to discount it. Keep only the sentences a dishonest seller couldn't write.

The "as written" lines here are composites assembled for this article, not quotations from any real listing or seller. Prices, mileages and dates in the examples are invented to make the rewrites concrete.
