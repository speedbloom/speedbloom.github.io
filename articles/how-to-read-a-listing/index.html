How to read a used car listing

Most of a listing is filler, a little of it is information, and a few phrases are doing quiet work you are meant not to notice. Here is a specimen ad we wrote ourselves, marked up line by line.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

The listing below is constructed. It is not a real advertisement, it does not describe a real car, and no seller wrote it. We assembled it out of the phrasing that turns up most often in used car ads so that every sentence in it could be annotated without pointing at anybody. Read it once straight through, then take the callouts in order.

Specimen listing · constructed for annotation

2019 Midsize Crossover AWD

$18,99511

89,412 miles · Automatic · Clean title7 · Silver

Runs and drives great.1 One owner,2 non-smoker.3 Recent trade-in.4 Just serviced.5 New tires and brakes.6 Cold AC. No accidents.8 Needs nothing.9 Priced to sell — must go this week.10 Financing available, all credit welcome.12 Call or text, serious inquiries only.13 No lowballers.14

  1. "Runs and drives"

    In the trade this is a category, not a compliment. It means the car moved under its own power onto the lot. It is the phrase used for cars that are being sold on the strength of moving, which is a lower bar than it sounds like. Paired with "great" it is marketing; standing alone on a cheap car it is closer to a warning.

    What does it do that you'd mention if I asked?
  2. "One owner"

    Verifiable, and worth verifying, because it is one of the few claims in a listing that a title history can confirm or contradict outright. It also means less than most buyers assume — one owner who skipped maintenance for six years is worse than three owners who didn't. Treat it as a fact to check, not a reason to relax.

    Is the seller the titled owner, or a dealer describing a trade?
  3. "Non-smoker"

    Cheap to say and easy to check in thirty seconds: the headliner above the driver's door, the sun visor, and the inside of the ashtray or the cubby that replaced it. An ozone treatment removes the smell for a few weeks and does nothing to the staining.

  4. "Recent trade-in"

    Usually true, and quietly useful. It tells you the car has been on the property a short time, which usually means nobody has driven it long enough to find its habits. It also tells you the seller's cost basis is a trade allowance, not a retail purchase — which matters more to their flexibility on price than anything else in the ad.

  5. "Just serviced"

    The most elastic phrase in the listing. It can mean a full service, or it can mean an oil change and a car wash. Neither is dishonest; they are just wildly different amounts of money. The word "serviced" carries no definition, so ask for the line items rather than the adjective.

    Can you send me the invoice for that service?
  6. "New tires and brakes"

    Check both, because both are visible. Tires carry a four-digit date code on the sidewall, and four matching new tires look different from two new fronts and two survivors. "New brakes" most often means pads only. Pads on worn rotors is a real repair that buys a year and then asks for the rest of the job. Neither finding is a dealbreaker — they are just price.

  7. "Clean title"

    This one is precise and it is load-bearing. It means the title carries no brand — no salvage, no rebuilt, no flood, no lemon designation. It does not mean the car has never been damaged. A car repaired privately, under an insurance threshold or out of pocket, keeps a clean title all the way through. Confirm the brand on the paper document rather than in the ad.

  8. "No accidents"

    Almost always a summary of a history report rather than a personal guarantee, and a report only knows what was reported to it. Cash repairs, small-shop work and anything settled between two drivers on the shoulder of a road never enter the file. This claim is worth nothing on its own and a great deal when a mechanic on a lift agrees with it.

    Is that from a report, or from your own knowledge of the car?
  9. "Needs nothing"

    No used car needs nothing. Every one of them is somewhere on the way to its next brake job, its next set of tires and its next fluid change, and a seller who says otherwise is either not looking or not telling. The sentence to hope for instead is a specific one: it needs rear pads in the spring and the passenger mirror glass is scuffed.

  10. "Priced to sell — must go this week"

    Urgency is the cheapest thing in any ad. It costs the writer nothing and it is designed to shorten your inspection, not their timeline. A car that has to go this week will still be there next week, and if it isn't, the deal you skipped was the one you couldn't check. Urgency is a reason to slow down.

  11. The price, with no "out the door"

    An advertised price is the vehicle only. What you actually pay adds sales tax, title, registration and — from a dealer — a documentary fee. Those are real money and they vary. The single most useful thing you can do with a listing price is refuse to compare it to another listing price until both have been converted into an itemized out-the-door total.

    What's the out-the-door number, itemized?
  12. "Financing available, all credit welcome"

    "Welcome" is not "approved," and a listing cannot tell you what any lender will do. What this line reliably tells you is that the seller has a finance relationship and would prefer you use it — which is fine, and which is also a reason to walk in already holding a rate from your own bank or credit union, so that the offer has something to beat.

  13. "Serious inquiries only"

    Filler, mostly — the ad-writing equivalent of clearing your throat. It occasionally signals a seller who has already had a bad week of messages, which is useful to know before you open with a lowball text. It tells you nothing about the car.

  14. "No lowballers"

    The only genuinely revealing thing about this line is that it appears at all. It says the price has already been tested by the market and the seller didn't like the result. On a car listed for a week that is noise. On a car that has been listed for two months, it is the most informative sentence in the ad.

What isn't in the listing

Read for absences as well as claims. A listing with twenty photos and none of the driver's seat, the trunk floor or the engine bay is telling you where not to look. No VIN means no history check before you drive out there. No mention of a second key is not sinister, but a replacement key with programming is a real bill, so it belongs in the price conversation rather than in a surprise.

And notice the shape of the description. The strongest listings we see are specific and slightly unflattering — the rear bumper has a scuff from a pole, the rear wiper doesn't park properly, everything else is sorted. Specificity is expensive to fake and it dates the writer as someone who has actually been under the car. A wall of superlatives with no defects named is not a lie. It is just an advertisement, and it should be read as one.

The three-message test. Send the VIN request, the out-the-door request, and the service-invoice request in one short message. A seller who answers all three plainly has told you more about the transaction than the entire listing did. A seller who answers none of them has also told you something.
