Buying

Dealer-speak, decoded

The car business runs on a private vocabulary, and most of it was never meant to be overheard rather than written to confuse. Twenty-two words, defined plainly, then explained in terms of what each does to the number you pay.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read
ABCDE FGHIJ KLMNOP QRSTU VWXYZ

A

As-is On the window
The car is sold with no dealer warranty — once it leaves the lot, repairs are yours.
As-is describes who pays if something fails on the drive home, not how good the car is. It makes whatever inspection happens before you sign the only inspection there is. How that works in New Jersey.

B

Back end In the F&I office
Everything sold after the price is agreed: service contracts, gap, tire and wheel, paint protection.
These carry margins the car itself could never support — hence the separate room and the separate person. Some are worth buying, and the test that costs you nothing is whether the offer is still there tomorrow.
Be-back At the desk
A customer who says they want to think it over and come back.
Said with a shrug — be-backs don't come back — it exists to justify urgency. A price real on Tuesday is real on Thursday; when it isn't, that tells you something.
Buyer's Guide On the window
The federally required window sticker stating whether a used car is sold as-is or with a warranty.
It is the sheet with the two big checkboxes, and what it says outranks anything said out loud. Read it before the test drive — it is the only document on the lot written for you rather than at you.

D

Dealer prep On the paperwork
A fee for cleaning and preparing the car for delivery.
It charges you for work the dealership had to do anyway to sell the car at all. Washing it and pulling the plastic off the seats is the cost of having a car for sale. We don't charge it.
Doc fee On the paperwork
A documentary fee for processing the paperwork of the sale.
Real work sits behind it — title, lien filing, the trip to the agency — but it is dealership revenue with an administrative name, not a tax. Ask for it inside the out-the-door total rather than at signing.

E

Etch In the F&I office
Etching the VIN into the glass, sold as a theft deterrent.
The etching costs very little to do; the charge frequently doesn't resemble that. Its distinguishing habit is arriving pre-printed on the paperwork before anyone mentions it, which is the part worth objecting to.

F

F&I In the F&I office
Finance and Insurance — the office you are walked to once the price is agreed.
It arranges your loan and sells the back end, and at many dealerships earns more than the sales floor does. Nothing improper is necessarily happening — but it is a second negotiation, beginning the moment you have mentally finished negotiating.
Finance reserve Behind the scenes
The margin a dealership earns by writing your loan above the rate the lender approved.
The spread over the life of the loan is shared, and the dealership's share generally arrives up front. The question that surfaces it is not what's my rate — it is what rate did the bank approve me at.
Floorplan Behind the scenes
The revolving credit line dealerships buy inventory with, secured against the cars themselves.
Interest accrues daily, per car, from the day it is bought to the day it is sold. It is why a car that has sat ninety days gets repriced instead of defended: a clock you cannot see, running.
Four-square At the desk
A worksheet split into four boxes — price, trade, down payment, monthly payment — negotiated at once.
Move one box and the others move with it, so a concession in the box you are watching can be funded out of a box you are not. The defense is to decline the format and settle the out-the-door price alone.
Front-end gross Behind the scenes
The profit on the car itself — selling price minus what the dealership has in it.
It is thinner than almost anyone guesses, and it is what the salesperson is paid on. When a dealership surrenders all of it, the money has been arranged to arrive from somewhere else. The full arithmetic.

G

Gap In the F&I office
Coverage for the difference between what your insurer settles on a totaled car and what you owe.
This is the one back-end product with a plainly visible trigger: you need it whenever the loan is larger than the car. On a long term with little money down, that describes the first several years.

H

Hammer price Behind the scenes
What a car sold for at a dealer auction, before fees, transport and reconditioning.
It is what people mean by dealer cost, and it is the smallest of several. The same car also carries an auction fee, a truck, the work it needed, and interest for every day it sat.
Holdback Behind the scenes
Money a manufacturer returns to a franchised dealer after a new car sells.
It means invoice is not really the floor on a new car, so a deal “at invoice” concedes less than it sounds. Strictly a new-car mechanism — an independent used lot has no manufacturer and no rebate arriving later.

P

Pack, the Behind the scenes
An internal charge added to every car's cost before anyone calculates commission.
It covers real overhead — the lot, the lift, the insurance, the phone. It also means the salesperson works from a cost figure that is not the car's cost, and can sincerely believe the deal is empty when it isn't.
Payment packing At the desk
Quoting a monthly payment that already includes products you have not agreed to buy.
The payment is set high enough to absorb them, so adding a service contract later appears to cost nothing. If you are given a payment, ask what it assumes: amount financed, rate, term, and every product inside it.

R

Recon Behind the scenes
Reconditioning — the work done to a car between acquisition and the front row.
Brakes, tires, an alignment, a battery, whatever the inspection turned up. It is the cost that swings hardest between two cars that look identical on a listing, and it is why the cheaper one sometimes isn't.

S

Spot delivery / yo-yo financing On the paperwork
Being sent home in the car before financing is final, then called back to re-sign.
If no lender takes the deal as written, you can be asked for a higher rate, more money down or a co-signer — by which point your trade may be gone. Don't take delivery until the financing is final in writing.

T

Trade differential At the desk
The gap between the price of the car you are buying and the allowance on your trade.
It is the only trade number that matters, and exactly where two figures can move in opposite directions to no effect. Price the purchase first, ask what your trade is worth second, and do the subtraction yourself.

U

Unwind On the paperwork
Cancelling a completed deal and returning everyone to where they started.
It happens — a lender declines after the fact, a title problem surfaces. It is not routine, and no general right lets you change your mind on a car purchase. Assume there is no undo.
Upside down / negative equity Behind the scenes
Owing more on the loan than the car is worth.
The reliable causes are a long term and a small down payment, and it costs you nothing until the day you want out of the car. Rolling it into the next loan doesn't retire it. Worked out in full.

What all twenty-two have in common. Every word above describes something that happens before you arrive or after the price is agreed — almost none of it lives in the part of the deal you were watching. The question that cuts across the whole list is still the boring one: what is the out-the-door price, itemized, in writing?

This glossary describes common industry practice in plain terms. It is not legal advice and states no particular lender's, manufacturer's or dealership's policy but our own. Practice and law vary by state; confirm any detail against your own paperwork.
