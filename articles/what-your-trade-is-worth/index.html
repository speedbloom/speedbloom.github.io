Selling

What your trade is actually worth

You will be quoted four different numbers for the same car, and the temptation is to decide which one is the honest one. They are all honest. They are answers to four different questions, and the useful skill is knowing which question you are actually asking.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

Take one car and hold it still: a six-year-old mid-size sedan, a little over eighty thousand miles, clean title, one small door ding, tires with maybe a season left in them, service done but not documented past the last two receipts. A completely ordinary car. Nothing about it is a judgment call.

Now run it past four different valuation lenses. Every number below is a round illustrative figure we picked to make the spacing legible — not a quote, not a lookup, and not what any particular valuation service, auction or online buyer would say. What matters is the shape of the spread, and the shape is remarkably consistent even when the dollars are not.

One car, four lenses

$9,500$15,000

That is a spread of $4,300 on one unchanged car — roughly a third of its own value — and not one dollar of it is anybody being dishonest. Here is what each lens is measuring.

1. Retail asking price

$14,500

The question: what would this car be listed at, ready to sell?

This is the number on a windshield after the car has been inspected, had its brakes and tires brought up to standard, been detailed, photographed, listed, and parked somewhere with insurance and a lift behind it. It includes the seller's willingness to stand behind it and the buyer's ability to walk away and come back next Tuesday.

It is also the number most people have in their head when they think about what their car is worth, because it is the one they can see on the internet. It is the only one of the four that is not an offer. It is an ask.

2. Private party price

$12,800

The question: what will one person pay another person?

Lower than retail because the buyer is absorbing everything the retail price paid for. There is no inspection they did not commission themselves, no recourse if the transmission gets strange in November, no reconditioning already done. They are buying the car and the risk in one motion, and the discount is what they charge you for carrying it.

This is usually the highest number you can actually collect. It also costs you the most in ways that do not appear on the check — listing it, answering the messages, the two people who do not show up, meeting strangers, handling the title yourself. We have scored that trade-off properly elsewhere.

3. Wholesale value

$10,800

The question: what does this car bring between dealers?

Wholesale is the price of a car sold in bulk, quickly, to someone who is going to do work to it. It assumes no detailing, no marketing, no waiting, and a buyer who is already discounting for the recon they cannot see yet. A dealer buying at wholesale is buying a project with a known shape.

This is the lens a trade-in offer is built from, which is why a trade number lands well under the retail price of the same car sitting three spaces down the row. It is the same car; it is not the same transaction. The gap between these two numbers is not profit — most of it gets spent before the car is ever listed.

4. A sight-unseen cash bid

$10,200

The question: what is it worth to someone who has not seen it?

Enter a year, a mileage and a handful of condition checkboxes and you get a number back in under a minute. That convenience is real and so is the price of it. Any bid made without laying eyes on the car has to be defensive: it prices in the possibility that "minor cosmetic damage" means a quarter panel, that the tires are worse than you think, that there is a warning light you have stopped noticing.

Which is also why these numbers move — sometimes a lot — at the inspection. A sight-unseen figure is a ceiling with conditions attached, not a price.

Why the spread exists at all

Three things account for nearly all of it, and none of them is anyone's negotiating posture.

Work not yet done. Between wholesale and retail sits reconditioning — brakes, tires, alignment, whatever the inspection turns up, plus the detail. The buyer of a wholesale car assumes that bill. They subtract it before they bid, and they subtract a cushion on top because they are estimating.

Time. A car that sells today is worth less than a car that sells for the right price in six weeks, because six weeks costs money — the money tied up in the car, the space it occupies, the value it quietly sheds while it sits. Every step down that ruler is partly a payment for speed.

Risk. Retail carries the most obligation and the most recourse; a wholesale buyer carries almost none; a sight-unseen bid carries the most uncertainty and prices it hardest. You are not just selling a car at each of those stops, you are selling a different amount of certainty.

What to actually do with this

Stop trying to find "the" value. Do this instead.

Collect two real offers, not four estimates. An estimate is a model's opinion. An offer is a number someone will honor. Get a written trade figure from somewhere and, if you are open to it, an online cash bid — then compare those two to each other, not to a retail listing you saw.

Price your own time honestly. If the private-sale premium over the best offer you hold is two thousand dollars, decide what two thousand dollars is worth against three weekends and a stranger in your driveway. Some people answer that easily in one direction, some in the other. Both answers are correct.

Separate the two transactions. If you are trading against a purchase, settle the price of the car you are buying before the car you are leaving is discussed. A generous-sounding trade number and a firm price can total exactly the same money as the reverse, and the version that feels better is not reliably the version that costs less.

The question that surfaces everything. Ask whoever appraises your car: "what would you have to put into it before you'd list it?" A serious answer — tires, front brakes, a detail, a windshield — tells you precisely where your number went, and gives you something to check. A vague answer tells you something too.

For what it's worth from our side of the counter: we would rather tell you plainly that you will do better selling it yourself than buy your car badly and have you find out in a month. On an ordinary car in ordinary condition, with some patience and a decent set of photographs, private sale usually wins on money. It just does not always win on life.
