Owning

Maintenance that actually matters

Every maintenance list you have ever been handed is sorted by interval. That sorting is useful to a service department and nearly useless to you, because it says nothing about which line to do first when the money is finite. So here is the same list sorted by consequence.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

One thing has to be said before the list, and it is not a formality. We are not telling you when to do any of this. Service intervals, fluid specifications and capacities are set by the company that built your car, they differ by model, engine, year and how the car is used, and they live in your owner's manual. Anyone quoting you a universal mileage figure — including a windshield sticker from a quick-lube — is quoting a policy, not your car.

What this page ranks is something the manual does not tell you: what actually happens if a line goes undone. A maintenance schedule treats every entry as equally obligatory. Reality does not. Some of these items, neglected, end the car. Some end a component. Some cost you money slowly, and a few cost you nothing but comfort. When the budget only stretches to three of them, that ordering is the only thing that matters.

01

Tires — pressure, depth, and age

Consequence: control

Four contact patches, each about the size of your hand, carry every input you give the car. Nothing else on this list has that job. Pressure is free to check and drifts constantly, especially across a New Jersey fall. Depth and age you can read yourself.

Stopping distance goes up, wet grip goes down first and goes down sharply, and a tire that is old enough for the rubber to have hardened can look fine and behave badly. This is the only item on the list whose failure mode is a loss of control rather than a bill.

02

Brakes — pads, fluid, and the noise

Consequence: control, then cascade

Brake pads are designed to be consumed and replaced. Brake fluid is hygroscopic, meaning it absorbs moisture from the air over time, and moisture is what lowers its boiling point.

A pad run past its friction material starts cutting the rotor, which converts a routine job into a larger one — that is the cheap version. The expensive version is a pedal that goes soft on a long descent because the fluid has aged. Brakes give a lot of warning, by sound and by feel, and almost nobody who gets hurt was unwarned.

03

The cooling system

Consequence: the engine, quickly

Coolant level, the condition of the hoses, the cap, and — above all — whether you are topping it off regularly. A cooling system that needs topping off has a leak, even if you never see a puddle.

This is the fastest route from a small part to a dead engine that exists on a car. A hose, a thermostat or a water pump is an ordinary repair. The overheat that follows one of them failing can warp a cylinder head or breach a head gasket in the space of a single exit ramp, and that repair can cost more than an older car is worth. If the temperature gauge climbs, pull over. Do not try to make the next exit.

04

Engine oil — level first, changes second

Consequence: the engine, slowly

Two separate habits get confused here. Changing the oil on the manufacturer's schedule is one. Checking the level between changes is the other, and it is the one people skip. Plenty of healthy engines use some oil between services; an engine that is low has no reserve when something goes wrong.

Oil starvation damages bearings and, on a turbocharged engine, the turbo — quietly, cumulatively, and without a warning light until it is far along. A replacement engine is one of the few bills that can exceed what a used car is worth. Checking the dipstick costs a minute a month.

05

The timing belt, if your engine has one

Consequence: potentially terminal, without warning

Some engines drive the camshafts with a rubber belt that is a scheduled replacement item; others use a chain that is not. Which one you have, and whether your engine is the kind where valves and pistons occupy the same space at different times, are two facts worth establishing on day one. Your manual and your mechanic can both answer them.

A belt gives no symptoms and then gives out. On an engine where the valves and pistons can meet, that is an instant, catastrophic internal failure from a part that was due for replacement on a schedule — the single most avoidable large repair in this whole list. This is exactly why we ask for the belt receipt on any belt-driven car we buy.

06

Transmission fluid

Consequence: a component that rivals the car's value

Practice here varies more than anywhere else on the list. Some manufacturers publish a service for it; some describe the fill as lasting the life of the unit; some specify a fluid that cannot be substituted. Read the manual for your car rather than applying a rule you heard about a different one.

Automatic transmissions rarely announce themselves early. When they do — a shift that hesitates, a flare between gears, a shudder at light throttle — the useful window is already closing. A rebuild or a replacement unit is the repair most likely to end an otherwise good car.

07

Steering and suspension joints

Consequence: tires, handling, inspection

Ball joints, tie rod ends, control arm bushings, the boots that keep grease in and grit out. These wear invisibly and are checked on a lift in about four minutes.

The routine outcome is a car that eats a set of tires unevenly and no longer tracks straight, which is money leaving slowly. The rare outcome, on a joint left long past worn, is a separation. In between sits a New Jersey inspection you fail.

08

Wipers, washer fluid, and every bulb

Consequence: seeing and being seen

The cheapest safety items on any car, and the ones most reliably ignored until the first hard rain in November or the first salt spray off a truck on the Parkway.

A smeared windshield at dusk in the rain is a genuine hazard that costs about the price of lunch to eliminate. A dead brake light is invisible to you by definition and obvious to the officer behind you.

09

Battery and charging

Consequence: stranding, not damage

Batteries degrade gradually and then fail on the first properly cold morning. Most shops will test one in a few minutes at no charge, and the test is far more informative than the age.

You get stranded. Unpleasant and inconvenient, occasionally expensive in tow fees — but it does not damage the car, which is precisely why it ranks here and not higher.

10

Filters, rotation, alignment

Consequence: money and comfort, over time

Engine air filter, cabin air filter, rotating the tires, correcting the alignment. Real maintenance with real value — and the part of the list that gets sold hardest, because it is easy to sell.

A tired engine filter and a skipped rotation cost you efficiency and tire life gradually. A cabin filter left in place costs you a musty vent and nothing else. Worth doing. Never worth doing before anything above it.

The question to ask at the counter

When a service advisor hands you a list, the useful question is not "how much?" It is: "Which of these is in the manufacturer's schedule for this car, and which is the shop recommending?" Both categories can be legitimate. They are not the same category, and a shop that answers that question cleanly and without irritation has just told you a great deal about itself.

The follow-up is the one from this page: if I do nothing about this line, what happens and how fast? An honest answer sounds like "the car will use a little more fuel" or "the rotor will get cut and the job doubles." A vague answer about the engine running better is not an answer.

What we do with this list. Everything in the top five gets checked and documented before a car goes out our door, and the findings are published in the inspection report rather than summarized. It is the cheapest honesty available to a small lot: the items that end cars are the items worth writing down.

No service interval, fluid capacity or part life is stated anywhere on this page, deliberately. Those figures belong to your car's manufacturer and appear in its owner's manual and maintenance schedule. This page ranks consequences, not timing.
