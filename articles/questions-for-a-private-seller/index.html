Buying

Questions for a private seller

A private sale has no Buyers Guide, no statutory warranty and nobody to call on Monday. What it has instead is the one person who actually drove the car, standing in front of you, willing to answer questions. This is a script for that ten minutes.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

We sell cars, so take this for what it is — but private-party buying is a real way to buy a car well, and people rarely get burned at it by the mechanical stuff. They get burned by a title that isn't in the seller's name, a lien nobody mentioned, or a story that didn't survive daylight. All three are findable by asking.

Listen to the shape of the answer more than the content. Someone who has owned a car six years knows things about it without having to think. Someone reciting a listing does not.

Why they're selling
Ask

"What's making you sell it?"

A good answer sounds like

Boring and specific. A third kid, a company car, moving into the city, a partner's car that became redundant. Real reasons arrive with detail attached and without being performed.

Walk away if

"Just don't need it anymore," flat, followed by a change of subject — especially on a newer car priced under the market. Nobody sheds a good car cheap for no reason.

Length of ownership
Ask

"How long have you had it, and did you buy it new?"

A good answer sounds like

A number of years and a place. "Four years, bought it used from a dealer in Toms River." Long ownership is the best thing a private car has going for it — the seller has lived with the consequences of whatever they did or didn't fix.

Walk away if

They've had it weeks, or they hedge. Short ownership often means an unlicensed reseller — a curbstoner — flipping auction cars from a driveway to dodge the rules a dealer follows. The tell is a seller with several cars, or a name that doesn't match the title.

The title
Ask

"Do you have the title in your hand right now, and is it in your name? Can I see it?"

A good answer sounds like

"Yes, it's in the house, I'll grab it." Then they grab it. You compare the name to their driver's license and the VIN to the windshield and the door jamb sticker. All of it matches.

Walk away if

The title is in someone else's name — a cousin, a business, an ex. That is either a curbstoner or a title that will not transfer. Never buy a car on the promise that the title is coming.

Liens
Ask

"Is there still a loan on it? Is anyone else's name on that title?"

A good answer sounds like

Either a flat no with a clean title to prove it, or a plain yes with a plan: "There's about $6,200 left, the bank holds the title, we'd do it at my branch." A seller who says so first is being honest, not warning you off.

Walk away if

They say no and the title shows a lienholder, or they suggest you hand over cash and trust them to pay the loan off afterward. Money and title change hands in the same room or not at all.

Service records
Ask

"Do you have records? Even a folder of receipts is fine."

A good answer sounds like

A folder, a glovebox envelope, a shoebox, an email thread from a shop. It does not have to be organized — it has to exist. Look for dated oil changes at sane intervals and one big-ticket item done properly.

Walk away if

"I did everything myself and never kept anything" on a 120,000-mile car. Not disqualifying — but it moves the whole burden onto your mechanic's inspection, so it has to be priced like it.

Recent work
Ask

"What are the last three things you had done to it?"

A good answer sounds like

Immediate and unremarkable. "Tires in the spring, front brakes last year, battery in February." Owners remember their last three repairs the way they remember their last three dentist visits — reluctantly, but accurately.

Walk away if

They can't name one, the three are all cosmetic, or the list quietly includes something enormous — a transmission, a head gasket — that the listing never mentioned.

Collision history
Ask

"Has it ever been hit? Anything at all, even a parking lot thing you paid for yourself?"

A good answer sounds like

A specific small story. "Someone backed into the rear door in a parking lot in 2023, I have the estimate." Minor repaired damage is normal. Volunteered minor damage is a very good sign about everything else they've told you.

Walk away if

A firm "never" from a car with mismatched paint texture, overspray on a rubber seal, or bolt heads that have been turned. The lie matters more than the damage.

Who did the work
Ask

"Who worked on it? Is there a shop you always took it to?"

A good answer sounds like

A name and a town, said instantly, with no hesitation about you calling them. A long relationship with one independent shop beats a thin dealer stamp history, and that shop will usually tell you honestly what the car has needed.

Walk away if

"A buddy of mine" for everything, with no receipts, on a car with obvious recent mechanical work. Also a bad sign: the seller getting tense at the idea that you might call the shop.

Whether it's been sitting
Ask

"When did you last drive it, and how often has it been driven this year?"

A good answer sounds like

"This morning." Or an honest account of low use with the consequences acknowledged — sat all winter under a cover, fresh battery, new tires because the old ones flat-spotted. Sitting isn't fatal; unacknowledged sitting is.

Walk away if

It's been parked eight months and it's already warm when you arrive. A seller who pre-warms the car has removed your cold start — which is when engines tell the truth.

The honest-flaw question
Ask

"If you were keeping it and had $500 to spend on it, what would you fix?"

A good answer sounds like

An actual answer. "Rear tires are getting close, and there's a rattle in the dash I never chased down." This is the best question on the list: it lets an honest person tell you the truth without having to admit to anything.

Walk away if

"Nothing, it's perfect." No used car is perfect and every owner knows their car's one annoying thing. "Nothing" means either they don't know the car or they've decided not to tell you about it.

Independent inspection
Ask

"Are you okay with me taking it to my own mechanic, at my expense, before I commit?"

A good answer sounds like

"Sure — when?" Possibly with a condition, like riding along or seeing your license first. Those are reasonable; they still own the car.

Walk away if

Any version of no. There is no good reason to refuse a buyer-paid inspection on a car you believe in. This question is worth more than the other eleven combined — and most buyers skip it because it feels rude.

Where you close
Ask

"If we agree on a price, can we do the paperwork inside my bank during business hours?"

A good answer sounds like

"That's easier for me too." A bank lobby gives you cameras, a teller who can verify funds, a notary, and a neutral room where nobody is in a hurry. If there's a lien, closing at the seller's own lender is better still.

Walk away if

They insist on cash, at night, somewhere else — or the price drops the moment you mention a bank. Honest sellers are fine with daylight and paperwork.

Two things to bring. Your phone, for photographs of the title, the VIN plate and the odometer — taken with the seller's knowledge — and a written bill of sale carrying both names, the VIN, the odometer reading, the price, the date and the words "sold as is." A private sale has no Buyers Guide and no dealer warranty. The paperwork you create is the only paperwork there is.

General information for buyers, not legal advice. Title transfer, sales tax and registration for private-party sales are handled by the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission — confirm the current process with them before you hand over money.
