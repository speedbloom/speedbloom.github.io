Owning

The first thousand miles

A used car will tell you most of what it has to say in the first month, and almost none of it on the test drive. Here is the month, laid out by the odometer, with what to do on one side and what to listen for on the other.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

A test drive is twenty minutes on roads you chose, in a car that has been sitting warm on a lot with someone friendly in the passenger seat. It is a useful screening tool and a poor interrogation. The real examination happens afterward, when the car gets cold-started in your own driveway, parked over your own concrete, and driven on the route you actually take.

So treat the first thousand miles as a scheduled inspection you carry out yourself, spread across a month. It costs nothing, it needs no tools, and it moves problems from month six — where they are yours alone — into week one, where a small lot can usually still do something about them.

Mile 0 · still on the lot

Before you drive away

Ten minutes here saves a phone call later. Everything on this list is easier to settle while you are standing next to the person who sold you the car.

Do

  • Photograph the car as you receive it — all four corners, the wheels, the interior, and a clear shot of the odometer. Two minutes now is the only record that exists of what it looked like on day one.
  • Check the VIN on the dash matches the VIN on every document.
  • Find the spare, the jack, and the wheel lock key if the car has locking lug nuts. A missing lock key is a trivial problem in a parking lot and a miserable one on the shoulder of Route 9.
  • Confirm how many keys you are getting, and take both.
  • Ask which fuel the manufacturer specifies, and check the filler door for a grade sticker.

Watch for

  • Warning lamps that light at key-on and then stay lit. They are supposed to sweep and go out.
  • A tire pressure light — worth resolving before you leave, because the cause might be a sensor or might be a tire.
  • Anything promised verbally that is not written on the paperwork. If it is going to be fixed, it belongs in writing before you sign.

Miles 1 – 50 · the drive home

The first deliberate drive

Take the long way, with the radio off and a window cracked. You are trying to collect a baseline, not diagnose anything. The point is to learn what normal sounds like on this car, so that a change in three weeks is obvious rather than debatable.

Do

  • One firm stop from around 40 mph in an empty stretch, with both hands loose on the wheel.
  • Full-lock turns in both directions at walking pace in a lot.
  • Get it to highway speed and hold it. Note the speed at which any vibration starts and whether it is in the wheel or the seat.
  • Run it over a rough surface — New Jersey supplies these generously — and listen to the suspension rather than feel it.

Watch for

  • A pull to one side under braking, distinct from a pull while cruising. They mean different things.
  • A steering wheel that is not centered on a straight road.
  • A clunk or click at full lock, which usually points to a joint or a boot rather than anything you did.
  • The smell of hot oil after you shut it off — a small leak onto a hot exhaust announces itself by nose long before it does by drip.

Miles 50 – 200 · the first week

Cold starts and a clean parking spot

This is the week the car stops being a lot car. It sits overnight, it starts cold, and it parks somewhere you can inspect. Both of those conditions reveal things a warm car on asphalt will not.

Do

  • Read the owner's manual, specifically the maintenance section. Your service intervals come from that book — not from us, not from a quick-lube windshield sticker, and not from what the last car you owned wanted.
  • Check the fluid levels on a cold morning and write them down. A level is only information if you have a previous level to compare it to.
  • Sweep your parking spot clean, or lay a sheet of cardboard under the engine overnight.
  • Set the tire pressures cold, to the figure on the driver's door jamb label, and do the spare too.

Watch for

  • Anything on the cardboard. Clear water from the air conditioning in summer is normal; color is not.
  • A coolant level that is lower than it was on Monday.
  • Noises that appear only on a genuinely cold start and fade after a minute — a brief rattle, a belt chirp, a rough idle that settles.
  • A battery that cranks slowly after two days parked.

Miles 200 – 500 · weeks two and three

Make every switch do its job once

Electrical and comfort faults hide because nobody exercises the systems. Spend twenty minutes making the car perform every function it has, including the ones you will not need until January.

Do

  • Walk around it with somebody working the pedals and stalks: brake lights, reverse lights, both turn signals, hazards, high beams, fog lights, plate lights.
  • Run the climate control through every mode — heat, air conditioning, defrost, recirculate, floor, face — and every blower speed.
  • Every window down and up, every door lock, the trunk release, the fuel door, the sunroof through its full travel, the rear defroster.
  • If it has all-wheel drive or a four-wheel-drive selector, engage it once in an empty lot so you know it works before you need it.

Watch for

  • Air conditioning that cools well and then quietly stops being cold.
  • A blower that works on the highest setting only — a common and cheap fault, but one you want found now.
  • A window that hesitates on the way up.
  • A rear defroster grid that clears in stripes.

Miles 500 – 1,000 · the first month

Hand it to your own mechanic

By now you have a month of observation, which is exactly the right thing to bring to an independent shop. Not to catch out the dealer — to start a relationship and establish a baseline you will be comparing against for years.

Do

  • Book an oil service or a general look-over at a shop you chose, and bring your list of small oddities rather than saying "check it out."
  • Start the file. Every receipt, every invoice, the inspection report the car came with, in one folder.
  • Note the odometer and the date on the day you bought it. That is the start line for everything that follows.

Watch for

  • A shop that finds a long list of urgent work on a car that has been driving normally for a month. Ask to see each item.
  • Brake feel that has changed since week one.
  • Any warning light that has come on and gone off again. Mention it even if it is not on now.

Tell somebody early

The thing worth saying plainly: if you find something in the first few weeks, say so in the first few weeks. Not because a used car sold as-is comes with a promise — it may not, and what your paperwork actually commits anyone to is worth reading rather than assuming — but because the practical willingness of any lot to help decays with time and mileage. A rear wiper that does not work, reported in week one, is a part and ten minutes. The same complaint at four months has become a conversation about who can prove what.

We keep the inspection report for every car we sell, which means a fault found in week one can be checked against what a technician wrote down before the car went out. That is the whole reason we publish the inspection instead of summarizing it. A car you can check is a car you do not have to take on faith, and the first thousand miles is when checking is cheapest.

One habit worth keeping past mile 1,000. Once a month, on a cold morning, walk around the car before you get in — tires, lights, the ground underneath it. It takes forty seconds and it catches nearly everything that gets expensive by being ignored.

Nothing here is a service schedule. Maintenance intervals, fluid specifications and capacities for your car come from its owner's manual or the manufacturer, and they differ by model, engine and year. This page is about observation, not specification.
