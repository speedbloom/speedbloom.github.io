Condition

What each noise means

Organized the way you actually experience it — by when the sound happens, not by which part makes it. Every entry lists more than one possible cause, because every one of them genuinely has more than one, and any page that hands you a single answer is selling you something.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

Two things make car noises hard to diagnose. The first is that a car body is a resonator: sound travels through metal and mounts and arrives at your ears from a direction that has very little to do with where it started. A noise that seems to be at the right rear is often at the left front. The second is that symptoms converge — several unrelated failures produce a clunk over a bump, and they are not distinguishable by description.

So use this as a way to narrow the question and to decide how quickly to ask it, not as a diagnosis. The useful discipline is to note four things before you call anyone: when it happens, whether it changes with engine speed or with road speed, whether it changes when you brake or steer, and whether it is there when the car is cold and gone when it's warm.

Book it This week Stop driving

In the first seconds after start-up

Cold, with oil pressure still building and everything at ambient temperature.

A short, hard rattle that clears after a second or two

This week

Classic complaint of a timing chain tensioner that bleeds down overnight and takes a moment to refill with oil pressure — but far from the only cause.

  • Chain tensioner
  • Hydraulic lifters bleeding down
  • Loose heat shield resonating
  • Exhaust manifold leak

A high squeal or chirp from the front of the engine

Book it

Belt noise, and it's louder cold and in damp weather because that's when a glazed belt slips most. Worth attention because a belt that lets go takes the charging and the water pump with it.

  • Glazed or worn drive belt
  • Weak tensioner
  • Seizing idler or accessory pulley
  • Misaligned pulley

A single loud clack, then nothing

Book it

Often just heat shield or exhaust movement as metal expands. Note whether it happens on every cold start or only after the car sat a long time.

  • Heat shield
  • Exhaust hanger
  • Engine mount settling

At idle, engine warm

Sitting in Park with nothing else happening — the quietest diagnostic environment you have, and the easiest to use.

A steady tick that rises and falls with engine speed

This week

Note that direct-injection engines tick audibly by design — injectors are noisy — so on those cars the question is whether the sound has changed, not whether it exists.

  • Normal injector noise
  • Exhaust manifold leak
  • Valvetrain clearance
  • Low oil level

A hiss or whistle you can hear over the engine

This week

Air going somewhere it shouldn't, in or out. On a turbocharged car it can be a pressurized charge pipe leaking, which will also make the car feel flat.

  • Vacuum line
  • Intake or charge-pipe leak
  • Crankcase ventilation fault
  • Brake booster

A deep, rhythmic knock, low in the engine

Stop driving

Heavy, dull, tied to engine speed, often louder under load. This is the one noise on this page that deserves no benefit of the doubt: shut it off and have it looked at before it is driven again.

  • Bottom-end bearing wear
  • Severe oil starvation
  • Internal damage

When you first move off

Walking pace, windows down, no radio. Thirty seconds that finds a lot.

A light metallic scrape that stops when you brake

This week

Many brake pads carry a small metal tab designed to contact the rotor and squeal when the friction material gets low — pressing the pedal pushes it clear, which is why the noise disappears exactly when you'd expect it to get worse.

  • Wear indicator
  • Surface rust after rain
  • Debris between pad and rotor
  • Sticking caliper slide

A clunk from underneath as you take up drive

This week

A single clunk when you move from Park to Drive, or on the first throttle application, is driveline lash — some is normal, a hard bang is not.

  • Worn universal joint
  • Differential backlash
  • Tired engine or transmission mount
  • Loose exhaust

When you brake

Grinding, metal on metal, felt through the pedal

Stop driving

Friction material is gone and something structural is contacting the rotor. Every additional stop is damaging parts and lengthening the repair.

  • Pads worn through
  • Seized caliper
  • Failed pad backing

A rhythmic pulsing you feel more than hear

This week

Pulsation that arrives with the brake pedal is usually about rotor thickness variation or deposits rather than a "warped" disc, whatever the shorthand says.

  • Rotor thickness variation
  • Pad material deposits
  • Wheel bearing play
  • Loose lug nuts

When you steer

Clicking or knocking on a tight, slow turn

This week

Rhythmic with wheel rotation, worse at full lock, usually only turning one way. Very often an outer constant-velocity joint whose boot split some time ago and lost its grease.

  • Outer CV joint
  • Worn strut mount bearing
  • Loose wheel

A groan or whine while turning at low speed

Book it

On hydraulic steering this is usually the pump complaining about fluid level or condition; on electric systems it is more likely mechanical.

  • Low power steering fluid
  • Pump wear
  • Binding strut mount
  • Sway bar bushings

Over bumps and rough pavement

New Jersey supplies the test surface at no extra charge.

A hollow clunk or rattle from one corner

This week

The hardest noise on this page to pin down, and the one most often chased with the wrong parts. A shop with a pry bar and a lift will find in ten minutes what a driver can guess at for months.

  • Sway bar end link
  • Strut mount
  • Control arm bushing
  • Worn shock or strut
  • Loose heat shield

A continued float or wallow after the bump

Book it

Not strictly a noise, but the same cause and worth recording: dampers that have stopped damping let the car keep moving after the road has stopped asking it to. They also scallop the tires as they go.

  • Worn shocks or struts
  • Broken spring
  • Failed damper mount

At speed, rising with road speed

The key distinction in this whole reference: does the pitch follow the speedometer or the tachometer? Put it in neutral and coast — if the noise keeps going while the engine drops, it follows the road.

A hum or growl that builds with speed, sometimes changing when you sway the car

This week

A load-sensitive hum — louder with weight shifted onto one side by a gentle lane change — points strongly at a bearing on the loaded side. But worn tires imitate this almost perfectly, so read the tread before anyone quotes you.

  • Wheel bearing
  • Tire wear pattern
  • Differential or transfer case
  • Mismatched tires

A whine that follows engine speed even in neutral

This week

If it stays with the tachometer when you coast, it belongs to something the engine turns rather than something the road turns.

  • Accessory pulley bearing
  • Alternator
  • Power steering pump
  • Transmission pump

After you shut it off

Ticking, hissing, and a fan that keeps running

Book it

Mostly normal: hot metal contracting, coolant settling, a cooling fan finishing its job. It stops being normal when there's a smell of coolant, a drip, or a fan that runs for a very long time after a gentle drive — which is a different article.

  • Normal cooling
  • Coolant leak
  • Failing fan control
  • Overheating event

How to hand this to a mechanic

The most useful thing you can do is stop trying to name the part. Say when it happens, what changes it, and what doesn't. "Hum from around the front, gets louder above forty, goes away when I ease left and comes back when I ease right, same whether I'm on or off the throttle" is worth more than an hour of a technician's guessing, and it costs you one attentive drive.

Better still, ride along. A noise you can reproduce in the passenger seat with the person who is going to fix it is a noise that gets fixed once instead of three times.

How we handle it here. Every car we take in gets driven with the windows down and the radio off, cold and then warm, before anything else happens to it. Noises that we find and fix appear as reconditioning line items in the published 168-point inspection; noises we found, investigated and judged normal get written down as exactly that, because "we heard it and here's what it was" is more useful to a buyer than silence.

General information for drivers and used car buyers, not a diagnostic procedure and not a substitute for inspection by a qualified technician. Every sound described here has causes beyond those listed, and urgency levels are general guidance — if a noise is accompanied by smoke, a warning light, a temperature gauge reading high, a change in braking or a change in steering, stop somewhere safe.
