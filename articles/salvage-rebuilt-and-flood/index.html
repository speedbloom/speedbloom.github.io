Paperwork

Salvage, rebuilt and flood titles

A brand is a permanent note the state writes on a vehicle's record. Each one means something specific, and each one has consequences that reach past the price into whether you can insure the car, borrow against it, or ever sell it again. Here they are in order of severity.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

Two things to establish before the list. First, brands are defined state by state. The names differ, the criteria differ, and a designation applied in one state is not guaranteed to be carried forward identically by another. Second, the decision to brand a vehicle usually begins with an insurer declaring a total loss, and what triggers that declaration is a function of repair cost against value under rules that vary by state and by company. We will not put a percentage on it. Anyone who does, on the internet, is quoting one state's rule at you as though it were everyone's.

Baseline

Clean title

No brand recorded. This is not a statement that the car has never been damaged — a car can be repaired privately, or repaired after an accident that was never declared a total loss, and keep a clean title throughout. Clean means nothing was branded, which is a lower bar than people assume.

Value
The market reference everything else is discounted from.
Insurance
Ordinary. Full coverage available on the usual terms.
Financing & resale
Normal. The widest pool of future buyers.
Disclosed

Manufacturer buyback / lemon law title

The vehicle was repurchased by its manufacturer after repeated unsuccessful repair attempts under a state lemon law. New Jersey has both a new and a used vehicle lemon law, and the used car statute scales its coverage to the vehicle's mileage and age — we are not printing those bands, because they have been adjusted before and can be again. Confirm them with the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs.

A buyback is not a damaged car. It is a car with a defect history that somebody could not resolve. Sometimes the defect was later fixed by a redesigned part; sometimes it was never really understood.

Value
Discounted, but the least severe of the brands here.
Insurance
Generally unaffected for coverage purposes.
Financing & resale
Some lenders decline. Resale is slower and needs explaining.
Disclosed

Theft recovery

The vehicle was stolen, the insurer paid the claim, and the car was later recovered. Whether a brand attaches depends on the state and on what condition it came back in. The range is enormous: a car found intact in a parking garage two towns over, and a car found on blocks with the interior removed, can both arrive under this heading.

The useful question is not "was it stolen" but "what came back". Ask for the repair documentation from the recovery, not the police report.

Value
Anywhere from a small discount to salvage territory.
Insurance
Usually available; some carriers ask more questions.
Financing & resale
Depends almost entirely on what the repair record shows.
Structural

Salvage

An insurer declared the vehicle a total loss and the state recorded it. In most places a salvage-titled car cannot legally be registered and driven in that condition — it is, in effect, a title for a collection of parts with a VIN attached. It can typically be repaired and re-titled, which is the next entry.

The brand says nothing about why. Collision, fire, vandalism, hail and water all arrive here. Two salvage cars with identical paperwork can be entirely different propositions, and the document will not tell you which is which.

Value
A large, permanent discount that never fully recovers.
Insurance
Not registrable as-is, so the question is deferred to rebuilt status.
Financing & resale
Conventional financing is generally unavailable.
Serious

Rebuilt / reconstructed

A salvage vehicle that has been repaired and passed whatever inspection the state requires before it may be titled and registered again. The inspection is generally aimed at confirming that the car is roadworthy and that the parts used in it were not stolen. It is not a quality assessment of the repair, and it is emphatically not a structural engineering review.

Some rebuilt cars are excellent — repaired properly, by people who cared, because the economics worked. Others are cosmetic work over damage that was never straightened. The difference is visible to a good body shop on a lift and effectively invisible in a listing. If you are considering one, the pre-purchase inspection is not optional and it should be done by a collision shop rather than a general mechanic.

Value
Substantially below a clean equivalent, permanently.
Insurance
Liability is usually obtainable; full coverage is where carriers differ sharply.
Financing & resale
Many lenders decline outright. Expect a small buyer pool later.
Structural

Flood / water damage

The one we would argue is the most dangerous of the set, because it is the one that hides best and gets worse with time. Water that has been through a wiring harness does not announce itself at the test drive. It announces itself months later, in connectors that corrode from the inside out, in control modules that fail intermittently, in airbag systems whose behavior in a crash nobody can predict.

Flood cars travel. A storm in one region puts damaged vehicles onto transporters headed for states that never saw the weather. Check the cabin's lowest points — under the carpet, under the spare tire, inside the seat rail channels — for silt, rust lines and a mismatch between a musty smell and a suspiciously new carpet.

Value
Heavily discounted, and difficult to appraise honestly.
Insurance
Comprehensive coverage can be hard to place.
Financing & resale
Poor on both counts. Problems often emerge after purchase.
Terminal

Non-repairable / junk / certificate of destruction

The end of the line. Depending on the state, this designation means the vehicle may only be dismantled or used for parts and may never be titled for road use again. A car under this brand is not a bargain project. It is scrap with a document.

If you are ever offered a car whose paperwork carries language of this kind, the correct response is to stop. Not to negotiate.

Value
Parts value only.
Insurance
Not applicable — the vehicle cannot be registered.
Financing & resale
None.

Title washing, and the one defense against it

Brands are recorded by states, and states do not all define or carry them the same way. Move a branded car across enough state lines and the note can become weaker, or disappear from the printed document entirely, while the damage that caused it stays exactly where it was. That practice has a name — title washing — and it is the reason a clean-looking title from a state with no connection to the seller deserves a second look rather than relief.

The defense is to check the VIN against the federal title information system that participating states report into, through one of its approved providers, rather than relying only on a commercial history report. The two are not the same product and they do not always agree. Reading both, and noticing where they disagree, is the whole technique. What a commercial report cannot see is its own subject — we covered that separately.

General information, not legal advice. Title brand definitions, the criteria that trigger them, inspection requirements for re-titling and insurer practices all vary by state and change over time, and nothing here is specific to your vehicle or your policy. Confirm anything that matters with the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission, the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs, or your own insurer, and consult an attorney licensed in New Jersey before relying on any of this in a dispute.

Where we stand

We do not buy branded cars. That is not a judgment about anyone who does — there is an honest trade in properly rebuilt vehicles, and for a buyer who understands what they are taking on it can be a reasonable decision. It is a judgment about us. Six cars at a time means each one has to be explainable in full, on a published inspection, to a buyer standing next to it. A car whose history contains a chapter we cannot document is a car we cannot stand behind, and we would rather have an empty space on the lot than that conversation.

Written for New Jersey buyers in September 2026. No total loss threshold, lemon law mileage band, age limit or valuation percentage is quoted on this page, because those are set by statute and by insurer policy and they change.
