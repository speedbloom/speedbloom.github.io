Paperwork

New Jersey inspection, what's checked

Most people assume the state looked the car over. The state looked at something much narrower than that, on purpose, and knowing exactly where the line falls changes what you do with a windshield sticker when you are buying a used car.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

New Jersey's periodic inspection program is run by the Motor Vehicle Commission, and for most non-commercial passenger vehicles it is fundamentally an emissions program. The mechanical safety portion that longtime New Jersey drivers remember — the lane where somebody checked your brakes and your horn — was discontinued for most passenger vehicles some years ago. Diesel vehicles and commercial vehicles are handled under different rules.

Scope, schedules, exemption periods for newly purchased vehicles and the network of state and licensed private inspection facilities all change, and we are not going to reproduce them here as though they were fixed. The register below is about categories — which parts of your car the program is aimed at and which it leaves alone. For the current requirement that applies to your specific vehicle, the MVC is the only source worth using.

In scope the program is aimed at this Not in scope nobody looks at it Check with MVC varies by vehicle or has changed

  • Onboard diagnostics and the check engine light

    In scope

    The heart of a modern emissions inspection. A scan tool connects to the vehicle's diagnostic port and reads whether the emissions control system is reporting faults and whether its self-tests have completed. A car with an illuminated malfunction indicator lamp is, generally, a car that is not going to pass.

  • Readiness monitors

    In scope

    The self-tests above have to have actually run. Clear the codes the morning of your appointment and the system reports itself as not ready, which is its own failure. Disconnecting the battery to make a light go away accomplishes the same thing. Drive the car normally for a while after any repair.

  • Fuel cap and evaporative emissions

    Check with MVC

    The evaporative system is an emissions system, and a leak in it is a fault the diagnostics report. Whether the cap itself is physically examined as a separate step depends on the procedure in effect. A cheap cap that seals badly has ended more inspection appointments than any other single part.

  • Exhaust system integrity

    Check with MVC

    An exhaust leak upstream of the sensors will affect what the emissions system reports, so it can surface indirectly even where the pipe is not separately inspected. Treat a loud exhaust as a repair question rather than an inspection question.

  • Brakes

    Not in scope

    Nobody measures your pads, your rotors or your parking brake. This is the single largest gap between what a sticker implies and what it means, and it is why a pre-purchase inspection is not redundant with a current inspection.

  • Tires

    Not in scope

    Tread depth, age, sidewall damage and mismatched sizes are not part of the emissions program. They remain entirely your problem and the road's opinion of them is unforgiving — reading a tire covers what to look for.

  • Steering, suspension and alignment

    Not in scope

    Worn ball joints, tired struts, a torn boot on a tie rod end: none of it is examined. A car can pass inspection on a Tuesday and be genuinely unpleasant to drive on a Wednesday.

  • Lights, horn, mirrors, wipers

    Not in scope

    Not part of the emissions inspection for most passenger vehicles — which is not the same as saying they are optional. Required equipment is still required equipment on the road, and a police officer is not bound by what an inspection station did or did not examine.

  • Seat belts, airbags and the SRS warning light

    Not in scope

    An illuminated airbag light is not an emissions fault, so it does not affect the result. It is, however, one of the most important warning lights in the car, and on a used vehicle it is a reason to ask what happened.

  • Rust, structure and prior collision repair

    Not in scope

    Nothing about the inspection speaks to whether a car has been hit, repaired well, repaired badly, or is corroding underneath. That is a lift, a flashlight and somebody who knows what they are looking at.

  • Diesel and commercial vehicles

    Check with MVC

    Different program, different procedures, different schedule. If you are buying a diesel pickup or anything registered commercially, look up the rules that apply to it rather than assuming the passenger car answer.

  • New and newly purchased vehicles

    Check with MVC

    There is an initial period before a newer vehicle's first inspection is due, and the length of it has been adjusted over time. Confirm your actual due date with the MVC when you register. Do not rely on what the seller believes.

  • Your registration and insurance documents

    In scope

    Not a test of the car, but bring them. An inspection appointment ended at the window for missing paperwork is a morning nobody gets back.

A passing inspection tells you what the car emits. It tells you nothing at all about whether the car is sound.

What that means when you are buying

A current sticker is worth something: it means the emissions system is functioning and the check engine light is off, which quietly rules out a category of expensive problems. What it does not do is stand in for an assessment of the vehicle. A car with new tires, honest brakes and a straight structure passes. A car with none of those things passes identically, and the two stickers look the same.

So use the sticker as one input and then buy the inspection you actually need — an independent pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic who works for you. If a seller resists that, you have learned the most useful thing available to you that day.

If a car fails

A failed inspection generally comes with a window to repair and re-inspect, and in some circumstances relief exists for vehicles where the cost of repair is unreasonable. We are not going to state the window, the criteria or any dollar figure attached to them, because those are set by the State and revised. The MVC publishes the current rules, and the inspection facility itself can usually tell you what your specific result means on the day.

General information, not legal advice. This page describes the general shape of New Jersey's inspection program, not the requirements that apply to your vehicle. Scope, schedules, exemptions, procedures and remedies are set by the State of New Jersey and change. Confirm everything with the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission.

Why we publish our own inspection

Because the state's answer and the buyer's question are not the same question. Ours is a hundred and sixty-eight points, and the list exists specifically to cover the ground the register above marks as out of scope: brakes measured, tires dated and read, suspension checked on a lift, structure looked at, every warning light accounted for. We publish it in full on each car, including the items we found and decided not to fix, with the reason. Six cars at a time is a small enough number that each one can be described honestly and individually — and a car that has to be explained away is a car we should not have bought.

Written for New Jersey buyers in September 2026. No inspection interval, exemption period, repair window or cost threshold is stated here on purpose — all of them are set by the State and change. Verify with the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission.
