Paperwork

Buying a car out of state

The car is cheaper, or it is the only one, or it spent its life somewhere they do not salt the roads. All good reasons. What follows is the sequence the purchase actually runs through, and the failure that hangs off each step.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

Two rules make the rest of this legible. The sale happens under the seller's state's rules. The titling happens under yours. Almost every problem below comes from someone assuming those are the same set of rules, or that one state's paperwork will satisfy another's counter.

Step 1 — before you travel

Read the title, remotely

Ask the seller to photograph the front and back of the physical title before you book anything. You are checking the issuing state, the brand or remarks area, the owner name, and the lienholder line.

Where it goes wrong

Brands do not always survive a move between states intact, so a title that looks clean in its current state may not be the car's whole history. Check the VIN against the federal title information system as well as a commercial report — see the brands reference.

Step 2

Establish who physically holds the title

The seller, or a bank. If a lender holds it, nothing can be completed at the moment of sale, and the timeline stops being about you.

Where it goes wrong

You drive several hours, hand over money, and go home with a promise to mail the title "once it comes." Now you are a creditor in another state. The mechanics of a payoff and release are worth understanding first — liens, and how they clear.

Step 3

Have it inspected where it sits

Hire a mechanic near the car, pay them directly, and get the report before you commit to the trip. Distance makes a pre-purchase inspection more important, not less.

Where it goes wrong

You inspect it after traveling. By then you have spent a day, a tank of fuel and possibly a hotel night, and the part of you that wants the trip to have been worthwhile starts negotiating with the part of you that just heard the words "some rust."

Step 4

Plan the trip around the calendar, not just the map

Confirm hours, confirm the seller will be there, and confirm the day works on both sides of the line. Note that New Jersey does not permit motor vehicle sales on Sundays — relevant in both directions if a dealership is involved.

Where it goes wrong

A Sunday round trip that could not have concluded in a sale no matter how well it went. Also: arriving after a bank's closing time with a payment method that needed a bank.

Step 5

Settle how money moves before you leave home

Agree the method in advance and use something traceable. Cashier's checks drawn on distant banks are harder for a seller to verify, and wires are irreversible.

Where it goes wrong

Wire instructions that arrive by email and are not what the seller sent. Confirm account details by voice, on a number you looked up yourself, every single time. This is the step where the largest amount of money disappears fastest.

Step 6

Insure it before you turn the key

Your policy, your VIN, effective from the moment you take possession. Handle it by phone from the seller's driveway if necessary.

Where it goes wrong

A long drive home in a car nobody has insured. Everything about that sentence is a bad idea, and it is an extremely common one.

Step 7

Work out how the car legally gets home

Options are typically a temporary tag or transit permit issued by the selling state, a dealer-issued temporary registration, or a transporter. Which are available to an out-of-state buyer varies — ask the seller's state motor vehicle agency rather than guessing.

Where it goes wrong

Driving home on the seller's plates. They belong to the seller, not to the car, and the arrangement collapses the moment anything happens. Never accept it, however casually it is offered.

Step 8

Understand the tax before you pay anybody

New Jersey collects sales tax on a vehicle when it is titled here. Some states also collect at the point of sale, sometimes at the buyer's home state rate. The arrangements differ, and so do the credits between states.

Where it goes wrong

Paying tax twice, or arriving at the counter having paid it nowhere. We are not printing rates or credit rules here because they change — ask the New Jersey Division of Taxation and the selling state's equivalent, before the purchase rather than after.

Step 9

Title and register it in New Jersey

The out-of-state title is surrendered and New Jersey issues its own. The MVC may require additional verification for a vehicle coming from another state, and what it requires has changed over time.

Where it goes wrong

Arriving with the wrong document set and losing the day. Call the MVC or read its pages first and ask specifically what is required for an out-of-state title — then go once. The ordinary New Jersey sequence is here.

The two failures that actually cost people money

Time

Every problem in an out-of-state purchase takes longer to fix, because the person who can fix it is far away and the document that solves it travels by mail. A lien release that would be a phone call locally can become weeks. Build that expectation in before you need it.

Tax

Not because anybody is cheating, but because two states' rules apply to one transaction and nobody at either counter is responsible for explaining the other's. Ask both, in advance, in writing where you can get it.

The honest version of the rust argument

Southern and western cars really do avoid the road salt that eats New Jersey vehicles from underneath, and a car that spent its life in a dry climate can be structurally cleaner than a local equivalent. That is a genuine reason to look out of state. But it cuts the other way too: regions that flood send water-damaged cars outward, and a long-distance transaction is exactly the kind where a buyer inspects less carefully than they would around the corner. The reason for the trip should survive the inspection, not replace it.

General information, not legal advice. Titling, registration, temporary tag availability, verification requirements and tax treatment are set by each state individually and change. Nothing here is specific to your transaction. Confirm with the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission, the New Jersey Division of Taxation, and the corresponding agencies in the state you are buying from.

We do this for a living, and it still takes planning

A fair share of our own inventory comes from outside New Jersey, because that is where the cars we want are. Even doing it routinely, with accounts and transporters and people we have used for years, the out-of-state ones take longer and need more attention than the local ones. That is not a warning against doing it yourself. It is a reason to do it deliberately — and if you are looking at a car three states away and want a second opinion on what the photographs are showing you, that is a call we are happy to take with nothing at the end of it.
