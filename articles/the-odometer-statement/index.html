Paperwork

The odometer statement

A number that costs nothing to change and moves the price of a car more than almost anything else. That combination is why there is a signed federal disclosure attached to it, and why the disclosure is worth reading rather than initialing.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

When a vehicle changes hands, federal law requires the seller to disclose the mileage in writing and the buyer to acknowledge it. On most modern transfers that disclosure lives on the title itself, in the assignment block on the reverse. Some categories of vehicle are exempt, and the exemption rules have been revised in recent years — so do not assume an older car is outside the requirement. Confirm the current position with the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission rather than with a memory of how it used to work.

Beside the mileage figure sit the status boxes. They do most of the real work, and almost nobody reads them.

Status: actual mileage
084 219 mi

The ordinary case. The seller is stating that the figure shown is the vehicle's true accumulated mileage. This is a statement of fact made under signature, which is precisely what makes it useful — and what makes getting it wrong a serious matter rather than a clerical one.

Status: not actual mileage
084 219 mi · discrepancy

The seller is stating that the reading does not reflect the vehicle's true mileage. This can be innocent — a cluster was replaced after a failure, a repair shop reset something it should not have — and it can be the residue of something that was not innocent at all. Either way, the notation follows the vehicle permanently and travels onto every title issued afterward. A car carrying it is worth less, is harder to finance, and is harder to sell again.

Status: exceeds mechanical limits
999 99 mi · rolled over

A survivor of mechanical instrument clusters, which ran out of digits and began again at zero. You will meet this mostly on genuinely old vehicles. It means the true figure is the displayed one plus a full revolution of the counter, and it is not a red flag by itself — it is a disclosure doing exactly its job.

Why the disclosure exists at all

Because mileage is the second most powerful input into a used car's value after condition, and unlike condition it is expressed as a single number that a buyer will accept without inspection. A mechanical odometer could be wound back with hand tools. A digital one is a value held in memory, and the equipment to rewrite it is neither expensive nor difficult to find. The protection is therefore not technical. It is documentary: a signed statement, preserved on the title, repeated at every transfer, creating a chain that can be examined afterward.

That is the whole design. Nobody is stopping the number from being changed. They are making sure somebody's signature is attached to it when it is.

What tampering looks like from the outside

A rolled-back odometer is a lie about one number, and the rest of the car goes on telling the truth. The evidence below is observational — none of it proves anything on its own, and all of it is worth noticing when several items point the same direction.

Wear that outruns the reading

Pedal rubber worn through to a shine, a steering wheel polished smooth on the upper rim, a driver's seat bolster collapsed on the outboard side. These are contact points and they wear on a schedule the odometer cannot edit.

Records that read higher than the dash

Oil change stickers in the door jamb, inspection documents, service invoices and tire shop receipts all carry mileage. Any one of them higher than the current display ends the conversation.

A history report that steps backward

Reported readings should climb. A drop between two entries is obvious. A long, flat stretch is subtler and worth asking about — though note what a report can and cannot see, which we covered in what the history report misses.

Replaced instrument cluster

Not sinister by itself; clusters fail. But a replacement should come with paperwork and, where required, a disclosure. Ask for both. Fresh screwdriver marks around the dash bezel of an otherwise untouched car are worth a question.

Consumables that do not match

A full set of tires, brakes and belts consistent with a much older, harder-working car than the number suggests. Conversely, original factory tires on a reading that should have consumed two sets.

A seller who volunteers the mileage first

Weak evidence and included honestly as such. But the number that gets mentioned before you ask, twice, on a car whose interior argues with it, is worth noting.

What to check, in order

  1. Read the status boxes on the title before you read the number beside them. Anything other than a clean actual-mileage mark changes the car you are looking at.
  2. Photograph the dash reading and the title reading together. They should agree, allowing for the driving done since the title was issued.
  3. Open the driver's door and read the service stickers. Check the jamb, the windshield corner and the underside of the hood.
  4. Pull a history report and read the reported readings as a sequence rather than as a verdict.
  5. Sit in the driver's seat and look at the three contact points — pedals, wheel rim, seat bolster — and ask whether they belong to the number on the dash.
  6. If anything disagrees, say so out loud and watch the answer. An honest seller with a replaced cluster has the invoice. That is the whole test.

If you think a number was altered

Odometer fraud is both a federal matter and a state consumer protection matter, and the remedies available to a buyer can be meaningful. We are not going to characterize them here — the specifics depend on facts we do not have and on statutes that change. Take the documents you already collected to the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs, and speak to an attorney licensed in New Jersey. The photographs from step two are the reason you will be taken seriously.

General information, not legal advice. This page explains how mileage disclosure generally works and what to observe. It is not a legal opinion, it is not specific to any transaction, and the exemptions, procedures and remedies in this area are set by federal and New Jersey law and change over time. Confirm current requirements with the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission or the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs.

What we do about it

We photograph the cluster the day a car arrives, before anything is touched, and that photograph goes into the file with the auction or trade paperwork. It appears again in the published inspection. Six cars at a time means every one of them is individually accountable, and a mileage question we cannot answer is a car we do not buy. That is less a moral position than an operational one: a number nobody can verify is a number that will cost us at resale, and we do not have inventory to spare.
