Condition

Buying a used EV

Most of an electric car is simpler than the thing it replaced: no oil, no exhaust, no timing components, brakes that barely wear. Nearly all of the risk has been consolidated into one component, and nearly all of the useful buying work is therefore about getting an honest, dated, comparable description of that component.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 7 min read

We are not going to print degradation percentages or range figures on this page. Any number we gave you would be an average of things that are not alike — different chemistries, different thermal designs, different climates, different owners — and you would then carry it into a negotiation about a specific car, where it would be worse than useless. What follows is the mechanism, and the method for getting the real numbers for the actual car.

What degradation actually is

Two things happen to a lithium-ion pack as it ages, and they are separate. Capacity fade means the pack holds less energy than it did when new, which is what people mean by "degradation" and what shows up as lost range. Resistance rise means the pack has become less willing to move energy quickly, which shows up as slower charging and, in extreme cases, reduced power — and it doesn't always track capacity fade.

What drives both: calendar time, which happens whether the car is driven or parked; heat, which is the most consistently damaging variable; time spent sitting at a very high state of charge; deep discharges; and high-rate DC fast charging, particularly when the pack is already warm. The design of the car's thermal management matters too — a pack with active liquid cooling has a meaningfully different exposure to a hot summer than one relying on air. That is a fact about how the car was engineered, and it's a fair question to ask before you look at any individual example.

One more thing worth knowing: degradation is usually faster early on and then flattens out. That means a young car with visible loss is not necessarily on a steeper trajectory than an older one that has settled — and it also means no one can extrapolate a straight line into the future from two data points.

Three numbers people confuse

The EPA label range is a test-cycle figure produced for a new vehicle under defined conditions. It is a comparison tool between models. It was never a promise about your commute, and it has nothing to say about a six-year-old example of the same car.

The range estimate on the dashboard — the "guess-o-meter" — is a rolling prediction built from recent energy consumption. Drive gently for a week and it rises; tow something up a hill and it collapses. It is a forecast of your driving, not a measurement of your battery, and reading battery health from it is the single most common mistake buyers make.

The state of health is the number you actually want: the vehicle's own estimate of present usable capacity relative to its original capacity. It is still an estimate, computed by software. But it is the only one of the three that is trying to describe the pack.

Reading a state-of-health readout

Where to get it

On some cars it's displayed in a service or battery menu on the car's own screen. On others it takes an OBD-II adapter and an app that understands that model's parameters. On most, a franchise service department can run a proper battery health check and print it. Ask for all three routes and use whichever the car supports.

What it is

A calculated estimate, not a direct measurement. The battery management system infers capacity from charge and discharge behavior over time, and it refines that estimate when the car experiences wide swings in state of charge. A pack that has only been cycled in a narrow band may carry a stale figure.

Comparability

Different tools and apps compute health differently, so two readouts on the same car can disagree without either being wrong. Compare like with like: same method, same tool, and always with a date. A screenshot with no date and no named source is not evidence of anything.

Cross-checks

Do not rely on one figure. Check it against the car's reported usable energy, against how much energy an actual drive consumed for the distance covered, and against how the charging rate behaves. A car whose health figure looks fine but whose fast-charging rate tapers unusually early is telling you something the single number didn't.

What it isn't

A prediction. Nothing measurable today establishes how much life remains, because the trajectory depends on how the next owner charges, parks and drives. It also isn't a warranty determination — whether a pack qualifies for coverage is decided by the manufacturer under its own criteria.

What to ask for

These are the requests, roughly in order of how much they settle. The wording matters less than the specificity; what you are trying to avoid is a conversation conducted entirely in reassurances.

  1. A dated battery health report, run while you watch

    Not a screenshot from last spring. Ask that it be produced during the viewing, from the car's own menu or by a franchise service department, with the date and the source on it.

    Can we pull the battery health readout together today, and can I have it in writing with the date on it?

  2. The charging pattern

    Daily charging habits do more to a pack over years than total mileage does. Some vehicles and apps record fast-charging sessions; where that data exists, ask to see it rather than to hear about it.

    What did you set the daily charge limit to? Did you leave it plugged in at full? How often did you use fast chargers, and does the car show a count?

  3. Where it lived

    Garaged or on the street, and in what climate. A car that spent its life in sustained heat has had a harder time than the odometer suggests. New Jersey is relatively kind on this axis — but plenty of used cars here arrived from somewhere that isn't.

    Was it garaged? Has it always been in this region?

  4. High-voltage service history

    Any module or pack replacement, any work on the pack's cooling loop, any high-voltage fault codes. A replaced pack is not a bad thing — it may be the best thing about the car — but it changes everything about the age of what you're buying.

    Has the pack or any module ever been serviced or replaced? Can I see the repair order?

  5. Remaining coverage, verified by VIN

    High-voltage components are typically covered separately from the rest of the vehicle, and the terms differ by manufacturer, model year and where the car was first sold. Do not accept anyone's summary — including ours. Call a franchise service department with the VIN and ask what remains and whether it transfers.

  6. Software, accounts and hardware

    Confirm the car is on current software and can still receive updates, that the previous owner's account and phone keys have been removed, that any connected services transfer, and that the charging equipment that came with the car — mobile connector, adapters — is actually present. Those adapters are not cheap to replace and are frequently missing.

The parts that have nothing to do with the battery

Tires. Electric cars are heavy and deliver torque instantly, and both of those consume tires faster than an equivalent gasoline car. Check that the fitted tires carry an appropriate load rating, and read the wear — the tread on a used EV tells you a lot about how it was driven.

Brakes. Regenerative braking does most of the slowing, so pads last unusually well. The flip side is that discs which are rarely used can corrode, particularly through a salted New Jersey winter, and a used EV sometimes needs brake work for rust rather than for wear. Look at the rotor faces and edges.

The 12-volt battery. Electric cars still have one, it still runs the computers and the contactors, and when it dies the car can be thoroughly stranded in ways that look far more alarming than the cause. Ask when it was last replaced and have it tested.

The charge port. Inspect the pins and the latch for damage and corrosion, and test that the car actually takes a charge — on an AC charger and, if you can arrange it, on a DC fast charger. Watch the rate it accepts and whether it holds it. Do this after a drive, when the pack is warm, because cold packs charge slowly by design and you don't want to misread a normal behavior as a fault.

And the New Jersey part

Cold weather reduces usable range — batteries deliver less when cold and cabin heating draws real energy — and the size of that effect depends on the car's heating design and on your commute. It is temporary; the range returns in spring. But if your winter drive already sits near the edge of what a used pack will do on a full charge, plan around the version of the car that exists in February, not the one you're test-driving in September.

How we handle it here. Any electric car on our lot is listed with a dated state-of-health readout, the tool used to produce it named, and the 12-volt battery load-tested — all inside the published 168-point inspection. Where the car can't produce a health figure we say that plainly rather than substituting an estimate, and we tell you which charging equipment is in the trunk.
