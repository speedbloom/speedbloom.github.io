Selling

Meeting a buyer

Almost everyone who answers a car ad is exactly who they say they are. The procedure below is not built on suspicion — it is built on the fact that a calm, ordered meeting produces a better sale, and happens to make the rare bad one much harder.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

The part of a private sale people dread is the meeting, and the dread is usually misplaced. What actually goes wrong in private sales is rarely dramatic: it is an unclear payment, a test drive that turned into an argument about price, or a title signed before money moved. All three are procedure problems, and procedure is something you can fix in advance.

Phase 1 — before you agree to anything

Screen by message

The people who will waste your Saturday declare themselves in writing first, if you let them.

  • Keep the first exchange on the platform you listed on. Moving immediately to a different app is a small signal worth noticing.
  • Answer questions about the car in detail and send the VIN if asked. A buyer running their own history report is a good buyer.
  • Decline anything involving shipping, a third-party escrow service, or an agent who will pay on someone's behalf. Not because every such arrangement is bad, but because none of them is necessary for a car someone can come and look at.
  • Do not give out your home address to get someone to commit. Give a neighborhood and a meeting spot.

The pointA buyer who is genuinely interested will ask about the car. One who is not will ask about the logistics of paying you.

Phase 2 — setting it up

Pick the place and the time

You choose both. This is not rude; it is normal, and any reasonable buyer expects it.

  • Daylight, and somewhere public with people and cameras — a busy retail lot is fine. Many New Jersey police departments maintain a marked exchange area in their parking lot; check whether your local department offers one.
  • Bring someone. They do not have to do anything. Their presence changes the tone of everything that follows.
  • Tell a third person where you are going and when you expect to be done.
  • Have the car clean, documents in a folder, and the title somewhere other than the glovebox.

The pointNothing here is adversarial. It is the same set of arrangements a careful buyer is making about you.

Phase 3 — the first ten minutes

Let them look

The most common seller mistake is talking. Hand them the keys to the doors, open the hood, and then stop.

  • Say hello, confirm the name matches the messages, and let them walk around the car without narration. People who are allowed to inspect freely trust what they find.
  • Point out the flaws you listed, unprompted, as they reach them. This costs you nothing — they are already in the ad — and it establishes the rest of what you say.
  • Offer the folder of records and let them read it.
  • If they want a pre-purchase inspection at their own shop, say yes. Arrange it for another day and go with the car.

The pointSilence during the inspection is the single highest-value sales technique in a private sale.

Phase 4 — the drive

Ride along, and drive your route

  • Before anyone gets in, photograph their driver's license — openly, with a sentence like "I'll delete this when we're done." Anyone who objects to that is telling you something.
  • Check that the name and photo match the person in front of you.
  • Go with them. Sit in the passenger seat. Do not hand over the keys and wait on a curb.
  • Have a route in mind — ten or fifteen minutes with a parking lot, some rough pavement, and a highway on-ramp. It shows the car properly and it keeps the drive bounded.
  • Whether your own policy covers someone else driving your car is a question for your insurer, and the answer varies by policy. Ask before the day, not during it.

The pointA structured route sells the car better than a wandering one, and it ends when you say it ends.

Phase 5 — the number

Negotiate standing up, outside the car

  • Know your floor before you arrive and do not recalculate it in the moment. Write it on your hand if you have to.
  • If they name a number far below your ask, ask what they are seeing that you are not. Sometimes the answer is real and useful. Often there is no answer, which is also informative.
  • Flaws you disclosed are already in the price. Say that plainly when they are recited back to you.
  • You are allowed to say "then I don't think this is going to work, thanks for coming." Ending a meeting politely is a skill worth having.

The pointThe advantage of a priced, honest listing is that you never have to defend the number — you only have to repeat it.

Phase 6 — the money

Move funds before you sign anything

This is the phase that actually matters and the one people improvise.

  • The cleanest version: meet at a branch of the buyer's bank during business hours. They withdraw or have a check issued in front of you; a teller can verify it; there are cameras and staff.
  • If it is cash, count it inside the bank and deposit it before you leave.
  • Be careful with instruments that can be reversed or that look official without being verified — personal checks, checks presented after hours, and consumer payment apps with reversal or dispute windows. If you cannot confirm the funds have settled, you are extending credit to a stranger.
  • Sign the title last. Funds confirmed, then signatures, then keys. Not in any other order, and not "to save a trip."

The pointAlmost every genuinely bad private-sale story is a story about signing before the money was real.

Phase 7 — after

Close it out the same day

  • Fill out a bill of sale in duplicate — date, time, price, odometer reading, VIN, both names and signatures, and the words "sold as is" if that is the deal. You keep one.
  • Complete the odometer disclosure and the title exactly as the document instructs. Do not leave the buyer's name blank; an open title is a problem that comes back to you.
  • Take your plates off. New Jersey's MVC publishes what a seller must do with plates, the registration and any notice of sale — follow their current instructions rather than an article's summary, including this one.
  • Do not cancel insurance until the transfer is genuinely complete.
  • Clear the car of you: unpair your phone, factory reset the infotainment, delete the home address from the navigation, take the toll transponder and the garage remote, and check the sun visor and the spare well.

The pointThe sale is not over when the car leaves. It is over when your name is off it.

If something feels wrong, leave. Not dramatically — you have a scheduling conflict, you will follow up tomorrow. You owe a stranger a polite exit and nothing else, and no car is worth standing in a situation you have already decided you dislike. In our experience that instinct is almost never triggered and almost always right when it is.

This is a general procedure, not legal advice. Title, plate, notice-of-sale and odometer requirements are set by the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission and change from time to time; confirm the current process with the MVC. Insurance coverage for a prospective buyer's test drive depends entirely on your own policy — ask your insurer.
