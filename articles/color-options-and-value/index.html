Buying

Color, options, and what holds value

There is no shortage of articles that will tell you a color costs you a precise percentage at resale. We are not going to do that, because an honest version of that number needs data we do not have. Here is the reasoning instead — which is more useful anyway.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

First, the omission, stated plainly. You will not find a resale percentage anywhere on this page. The figures that circulate for this come from proprietary transaction databases, cover specific model years and segments, and get repeated years later stripped of every qualifier that made them meaningful. Quoting one would look authoritative and would be, in our hands, made up.

What we can offer is the structure underneath. Every option and every color affects resale through the same three channels, and once you can see the channels you can work out the answer for the specific car in front of you — which is the only car that matters.

Q1

Does it widen or narrow the buyer pool?

Resale value is mostly liquidity. A feature wanted by most buyers sells quickly at a normal price; one wanted by few sells slowly or cheaply.

Q2

Can it be added later, and at what cost?

Things that cannot be retrofitted keep their value best. Anything easily added afterward is worth little as a factory option at resale.

Q3

What does it cost to keep?

Some features carry an ongoing bill — maintenance, insurance, consumables, or the risk of a failure that makes the car harder to sell.

Nine common cases, run through the same three questions. The verdict on each is a direction, not a figure.

Conventional exterior colors

Holds
Q1The widest possible pool. White, black, silver and gray offend nobody and are what most shoppers filter for by default, which is the only thing "popular" means here.
Q2Effectively impossible to change. A respray is expensive, visible on inspection, and reduces value rather than adding it.
Q3Neutral. Dark colors show swirls and need more care to look good; light ones hide both dirt and minor damage.

Bold or unusual exterior colors

Costs time, not always money
Q1Narrower pool by definition. The effect is usually on how long it takes to sell rather than on the price a determined buyer will pay — and on some enthusiast models a rare color does the opposite and enlarges the pool.
Q2Same as above — permanent.
Q3Some specialty finishes cost meaningfully more to repair properly after a collision. Worth knowing before, not after.

Light interior upholstery

Costs
Q1Slightly narrower, and it degrades with age in a way exterior color does not. A worn light interior reads as "hard life" even when the car has had an easy one.
Q2Not practically changeable. Reupholstery costs more than the value it restores on an ordinary car.
Q3Ongoing effort. This is the option whose resale outcome is most in the owner's control — the same interior can present as immaculate or as finished.

All-wheel drive, in New Jersey

Holds
Q1Wider pool here than in most of the country, and noticeably wider in the weeks after the first real snow. Regional demand is real and seasonal.
Q2Impossible to add. It is a drivetrain, not a feature.
Q3Genuine carrying cost — more driveline components to service, tires that generally must be replaced as a set, and usually worse fuel economy.

Sunroof or panoramic roof

Neutral, with a tail risk
Q1Slightly wider — most buyers like one, few refuse a car without one.
Q2Not retrofittable in any way you would want.
Q3The risk item. Drains clog, seals age, and a leaking roof damages a headliner and an interior. Check the drains and look for staining at the A-pillars; a known-good one is worth having, a suspect one is a liability.

Large wheels with low-profile tires

Costs
Q1Mildly wider on appearance, narrower among the buyers who know what the replacement tires cost.
Q2Easily changed either direction, which is exactly why it adds little at resale.
Q3The clearest carrying cost on this list: more expensive tires, more frequent wheel damage on New Jersey roads, and a harsher ride that some buyers walk away from.

Factory navigation and older infotainment

Costs
Q1Was a premium option when new; ages badly. A phone does the job better and buyers know it.
Q2Here the logic inverts — phone integration can often be added aftermarket, so the factory system's advantage evaporates.
Q3Screens and control modules are expensive to replace and increasingly hard to source on older cars. Test every function before you buy.

Heated seats, heated steering wheel, remote start

Holds, quietly
Q1Genuinely wide appeal in this climate, and cheap to want. They rarely make a sale on their own but they close one.
Q2Awkward and rarely worth retrofitting well, which preserves the factory version's value.
Q3Low. Simple, durable, occasionally a failed element or relay.

Aftermarket modifications

Costs, reliably
Q1Sharply narrower. The pool shrinks to buyers who want that exact taste, and expands the pool of buyers who now wonder how the car was driven.
Q2Reversible in principle, and the reversal costs money nobody will reimburse.
Q3Potential effect on inspection, insurance and any remaining coverage. This is the one category where money spent is most consistently money gone.

What the framework says overall

Two patterns fall out of running the questions. The things that hold value are the things you cannot add later — drivetrain, body style, color, seating configuration. Anything a future owner could install in an afternoon is worth roughly what that afternoon costs, no matter what it cost as a factory box on the original window sticker.

And what people call resale value is usually days-to-sell in disguise. An unusual car does not become worthless; it becomes slow. If you are the kind of owner who will happily wait six weeks for the right buyer, an unpopular configuration bought at a discount is a perfectly rational purchase — you are being paid for patience. If you need to move it in a week, the popular one was the cheaper choice all along.

How to use this on a specific car. Take the option you are paying extra for and ask the three questions out loud. If the answers are "most people want it", "it can't be added later" and "it costs nothing to keep", pay for it. If any answer goes the other way, you are buying it for yourself rather than for the next owner — which is fine, as long as you know that's the trade.

This article deliberately contains no resale percentages, dollar figures or depreciation rates for any color or option. Reliable numbers of that kind come from transaction datasets we do not hold, are specific to model, year and region, and lose their meaning when repeated out of context. Nothing here describes any manufacturer's option packaging or any insurer's rating practice.
