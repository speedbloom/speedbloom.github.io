Owning

Finding a mechanic you can keep

Nobody chooses a shop well, because everybody chooses one at the worst possible moment — on a Tuesday, with a car that will not start and a day that has already gone wrong. The fix is to choose one while nothing is broken, using things you can watch a shop do.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

Reviews are close to useless here. A five-star oil change tells you almost nothing about how a shop behaves on a $1,800 diagnosis, and the angriest review on any shop's page is usually from somebody who was told something true. What you want instead is evidence you gather yourself, in a specific order, starting with a phone call and ending with the first job that actually costs money.

Go looking now, while the stakes are an oil change.

Encounter one

The phone call

Four minutes, before you have spent a dollar. You are not booking anything yet; you are listening to how they handle an ordinary conversation.

See
They ask what the car is doing, not what you want done.
Means
A shop that takes orders will sell you the part you asked for. A shop that diagnoses wants symptoms first — when it happens, cold or warm, braking or turning, at what speed.
Test
Describe something vaguely: "it makes a noise when I turn." Count the follow-up questions.
See
They state a labor rate and a diagnostic fee without flinching.
Means
Diagnosis is skilled work and it costs money. A shop that pretends it is free is recovering it somewhere you cannot see.
Test
"What's your hourly rate, what do you charge to diagnose, and does that apply toward the repair if I go ahead?"
See
They can name what they don't do.
Means
"We don't rebuild transmissions here, we use a shop in Freehold" is a shop telling you the truth about its own capability. Everybody-does- everything is rarely true, and finding out mid-job is expensive.
Test
Ask what they send out, and who they send it to.
See
They have people qualified for your kind of car.
Means
ASE certification is a technician credential in specific areas, and shops with it generally say so. Diesel, hybrid and European work often need particular training and tooling.
Test
"Who would work on mine, and what are they certified in?"

Encounter two

The first small job

Take them something trivial on purpose — an oil service, a coolant check, a state inspection. You are buying an audition, and it is the cheapest one you will ever run.

See
The mileage is on the invoice without you asking.
Means
They are writing records, not receipts. That habit is worth real money to you years later when you sell the car.
Test
Read the invoice in the parking lot. Date, mileage, parts named, labor separated.
See
They found something and did not do it.
Means
This is the single most encouraging signal on the page. A shop that reports a worn item, prices it, and leaves the decision with you is treating you as the owner of the car.
Test
Ask which of their findings can wait and which cannot. A good answer is specific about consequence.
See
The car comes back the way it went in.
Means
No grease on the wheel, the seat where you left it, the floor mat not soaked. Small discipline is observable and it correlates with the discipline you cannot observe.
Test
Look at the steering wheel and the door edge before you drive off.
See
The shop floor is organized rather than clean.
Means
Working shops are dirty; that is fine. What you are looking for is cars supported properly, tools where they belong, and nothing pooled under a lift. Also glance at the parking lot — the same three cars sitting for months usually means stalled jobs.
Test
Ask to see your car on the lift. A shop comfortable with that request is telling you something.

Encounter three

The first bill that matters

Eventually something real goes wrong. This is where a shop becomes one you keep, or one you used to use.

See
The invoice matches the estimate, or a phone call happened first.
Means
Authorization before work is the boundary. A shop that crosses it once will cross it again, and a surprise on the final bill is a policy, not an accident.
Test
At drop-off: "Call me before you exceed the estimate by anything." Then see what happens.
See
They explain the diagnosis, not only the repair.
Means
"Replaced the sensor" is a line item. "The code pointed at the sensor, we tested the circuit first because the wiring fails here, the sensor was the fault" is a shop that thinks — and that will not sell you the same part twice.
Test
Ask to see the old part. Nearly every good shop will keep it if you ask in advance.
See
They triage the rest of the car.
Means
A sorted list — do now, do this year, watch it — is the most valuable thing a shop produces, and the thing that separates a mechanic from a parts-changer.
Test
"If this were your car and your money, what order would you do these in?"
See
How they behave when something comes back.
Means
The most informative event in the whole relationship. Everyone is pleasant when the repair works. A shop that takes the car straight back in, without argument and without a new charge for the same fault, has just told you everything.
Test
Ask up front what they warrant their own work for, and get the answer written on the invoice rather than said at the counter.

What sends you elsewhere

  • A long list of urgent work on a car that has been driving normally. Not proof of anything by itself — ask to be shown each item on the lift. The reaction to that request is the actual test.
  • Refusing to put a quote in writing. No explanation makes this acceptable.
  • "While we were in there." Work you did not authorize, presented as a favor, on a bill you are now holding.
  • Pressure to decide today on a car that is safe to drive. Urgency is occasionally real, and when it is, a good shop explains the consequence instead of the deadline.
  • Vague answers about parts. You are entitled to know whether a part is new, remanufactured or used, and which it is changes both the price and the expectation.

Why a dealership is telling you to go find a mechanic. Two reasons, both self-interested enough to state. Customers with their own shop keep their cars alive, which means their trade-ins are worth something when they come back. And we want you to take any car you are considering — including ours — to a mechanic of your own before you buy it. A lot that encourages that is making a claim about its cars that it has to be willing to have checked.

Nothing here states what any shop must do, what a certification requires, or what a repair warranty covers. Those are matters for the shop, the certifying body and the written terms you are given.
