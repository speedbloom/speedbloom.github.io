Selling

Private sale or trade-in

We buy cars, so treat everything below with appropriate suspicion — and then notice that the scorecard does not come out in our favor on the criterion most people care about most.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

There are two ways your old car leaves your possession: someone who wants to drive it buys it from you, or someone who wants to resell it buys it from you. Those are genuinely different businesses and they produce genuinely different outcomes, which is why the answer to "which is better" is a scorecard and not a sentence.

Six criteria, each scored out of five for each route. The scores are our judgment, not a measurement of anything — argue with them. What we would ask is that you argue with them criterion by criterion rather than deciding the whole question on the first one.

CriterionPrivateTrade
Money you keep

Not close, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. A private buyer pays something near retail; a dealer pays something near wholesale, because a dealer has to recondition, list, insure and warrant the car afterward. On an ordinary car in ordinary shape the difference is real money. The four valuation lenses explain where it goes.

Effort it costs you

Photographs, a listing, a phone number you have now given to the internet, messages that open with "is it still available," two people who do not show up, a test drive with a stranger, and the paperwork at the end. A trade is a signature. This criterion is the entire case for trading and it is a stronger case than people admit until they have done the other thing once.

Time to done

A trade is finished the day you sign. A private sale takes as long as it takes — usually weeks, occasionally a weekend, sometimes long enough that you reprice it. The car keeps aging and keeps costing you insurance and registration while it waits.

Risk

Private sale carries payment risk, a stranger-in-your-driveway component, and the small but real possibility of a buyer who comes back unhappy. Most of that is manageable with procedure rather than luck — see meeting a buyer — but "manageable" is not "absent," and a trade has essentially none of it.

Paperwork

Title signed correctly, bill of sale, plate and registration handled, and — if there is a loan on it — a payoff sequence that has an awkward middle where nobody is holding the title. A dealership does that part every day. You will do it twice in your life.

Sales tax on the next car

In some states a trade-in allowance reduces the amount of the new purchase that sales tax is calculated on, which quietly narrows the gap in the first row. Whether and how that applies to your particular deal is not something to take from an article — confirm it against the New Jersey Division of Taxation's guidance or ask for it to be shown on the worksheet. When it applies, it matters; when people forget it exists, they compare the wrong two numbers.

And that total is close to meaningless, which is the point of showing it. Adding those scores treats "money you keep" and "paperwork" as equally important, and for almost nobody are they. The scorecard is not a verdict — it is a way of finding out which row you actually care about.

Weight it yourself, in one step

Get a real written offer for your car. Then estimate, honestly, what you would net selling it privately — not the highest asking price you can find online, but what a similar car in your condition actually changes hands for. Subtract. The difference is the fee you are paying for every other row on the scorecard.

Now divide that number by the hours you think the private sale would cost you, start to finish, including the two no-shows. If the answer is a wage you would happily work for, sell it yourself. If it is not, trade it and stop agonizing.

The honest verdict

On an ordinary, sound, easy-to-sell car — a common model, clean title, reasonable mileage, no mechanical drama — you will do better selling it privately. That is not a close call and we are not going to pretend it is. If you have the patience, the parking space and a weekend to spare, take the money.

Where trading genuinely wins

The car is hard to sell. High mileage, an unusual model, a branded title, cosmetic damage, or something that needs work you do not want to do. Private buyers for those cars exist but they are few and they negotiate hard, and the listing sits. The gap between a trade offer and a realistic private price narrows sharply the less appealing the car is.

You still owe on it. A lien turns a private sale into a three-party transaction with a period where the payoff has been sent and the title has not arrived. It is entirely doable — the sequence is here — but it is the single most common place a private sale falls apart, and a dealership absorbs that step as routine.

You need it gone on a specific day. Moving, a lease ending, a job starting somewhere the car cannot come. Certainty has a price and sometimes the price is fair.

The car is a liability you would rather not hand a neighbor. If you know the transmission is on borrowed time, selling it to a private buyer means disclosing that and pricing it accordingly. Plenty of people would rather hand that car to someone who does this for a living.

One thing that is not a reason

"They gave me so much for my trade." Your trade allowance and the price of the car you are buying are two numbers in one arithmetic, and they can be moved against each other freely to produce the same total. A big trade number against a firm price and a small trade number against a discounted price can cost you exactly the same money, and only one of them feels good.

The defense is to settle the price of the car you are buying — out the door, itemized — before your car is appraised at all. If the trade figure moves the purchase price afterward, you have learned something useful about the deal.

Scores on this page are our editorial judgment, not measurements, and the totals are deliberately unweighted to show how little a total means. Tax treatment of trade-in allowances varies and changes; confirm yours with the New Jersey Division of Taxation or a tax professional rather than with us.
