Selling

How to photograph a car

Fifteen frames, in order. The first eight sell the car; the next four answer questions before they are asked; the last three are the ones that separate a listing people trust from a listing people scroll past.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

Photographs are the entire first round of the sale. Before anyone reads your description or your price they have already decided, from four thumbnails, whether this is a car owned by a careful person. That judgment is mostly unconscious and almost entirely about framing, light and what you chose to show.

Two rules before the list. Wash the car first — photographs of a dirty car waste good light and no amount of framing recovers it. And shoot in soft light: overcast midday, or the hour before sunset. Hard noon sun produces black shadows under the car and blown-out highlights on the hood, which hides exactly the paint a buyer is trying to assess. Hold the phone horizontally, crouch to about headlight height, and back up further than feels right.

Set one — the eight that sell it

Shoot these in one pass, same location, same light, wheels turned slightly toward you on the front so the face of the wheel shows.

01Three-quarter front
driver's side

The hero

Stand off the front corner so you see the front and the full driver's side in one frame. This is your thumbnail and it does more work than the other fourteen combined. Whole car in frame with air around it, nothing cropped, horizon level.

What buyers readWhether the car looks like the car in the title of the ad — and whether you know what you're doing.
02Three-quarter rear
passenger side

The opposite corner

The mirror image of the first. Between them, every exterior panel has been shown from an angle, which is the point. A listing with two front three-quarters and no rear angle looks like it is avoiding something.

What buyers readThat all four corners exist and none of them is being kept out of shot.
03Full profile
square to the side

The side

Directly perpendicular, centered on the car, far enough back that the wheels are not distorted. This is the frame where ride height, panel gaps and any sag or mismatch show up. Shoot it flat and honest.

What buyers readStance, gaps, and whether the reflections run straight down the flank.
04Straight-on front

The face

Square to the grille, centered. Shows bumper alignment, headlight clarity and whether the two lights have aged the same amount — a mismatched pair is a quiet sign one was replaced.

What buyers readFront-end damage history, mostly without knowing that's what they're looking at.
05Straight-on rear

The back

Same treatment. Include the exhaust tips and the trunk seam. If there is a hitch, a dealer sticker or a bumper scuff, this is where it belongs — shown rather than discovered.

What buyers readTowing use, and whether the bumper has been off.
06One front wheel
tread visible

The tire

Crouch, fill the frame with one wheel, and angle it so the tread face is actually visible, not just the sidewall. Curb rash, if there is any, goes in this shot rather than being hidden by a clever angle.

What buyers readHow much money they need to spend in the first month.
07Hood and roof
from slightly above

The paint, honestly

Stand a little higher than the hood and shoot down the length of it, with something straight in the background — a building edge, a fence line. The reflection of a straight line across the paint tells the truth about clear coat, swirls and any wave in the panel.

What buyers readClear coat condition, which is the one exterior problem that is expensive and cannot be undone.
08Engine bay
hood fully up

Under the hood

Wipe the dust off. Do not steam clean it — an immaculate bay on a high-mileage car reads as staged, and anyone experienced wonders what leak got washed away. Even light, no flash, whole bay in frame.

What buyers readLeaks, aftermarket surprises, and whether the car has been cared for or merely presented.

Set two — the four that answer questions

Every one of these exists to close a question a buyer would otherwise have to ask you, and asking is friction.

09Driver's seat
from the open door

The seat

Stand outside the open driver's door and shoot the seat square-on, including the outer bolster and the pedal rubbers below it. The bolster and the pedals are where mileage actually shows, and a buyer comparing them against your odometer is doing something sensible.

What buyers readWhether the wear matches the miles.
10Dash and cluster
engine running

The cluster, lit

Start the car, let the self-test lamps go out, then shoot the instrument cluster so the odometer reads clearly and no warning lights are on. A photograph taken with the ignition on but the engine off shows every lamp illuminated and is useless — worse, it looks evasive.

What buyers readThe mileage, confirmed, and no lights — the single most reassuring frame in the set.
11Rear seats and floor

The back

Door open, full bench in frame, floor mats visible. If there are child seat anchors, show them. If the back has lived a hard life, showing it now is cheaper than having it discovered at the meeting.

What buyers readWhether the car carried kids, dogs, or nothing at all.
12Trunk, empty
floor lifted

The trunk and what's under it

Empty it, then lift the floor and show the spare, the jack and the well. A dry, clean spare well is quiet evidence of no rear-end water intrusion; a missing jack is a thing buyers notice at the worst possible moment.

What buyers readWater history and whether the tools are all there.

Set three — the three most sellers skip

These are the frames that make the other twelve believable.

13Every flaw
close and in focus

The damage shots

One frame per flaw: the door ding, the bumper scuff, the curbed wheel, the tear in the seat, the chip in the windshield. Close, sharp, well lit, and captioned in the listing. This feels like sabotage and is the opposite.

A buyer who sees four honest flaw photographs stops hunting for a fifth. A buyer who sees none assumes you are hiding something and arrives looking for it — and finds it, and now every other claim you made is in question.

What buyers readThat the rest of the listing is probably true.
14Both keys
on the seat

The keys

Two keys, side by side. Replacing a modern key with its programming is not a trivial bill, and a photograph of both is worth more to a buyer's confidence than a sentence claiming it.

What buyers readOne less deduction to ask for.
15Service folder
fanned out

The records

Spread the receipts so a few dates and shop names are legible and shoot them flat on a table. You are not asking anyone to read them; you are showing that they exist and that you can produce them.

Do not photograph the title, and crop or blur the license plate. A buyer needs to see that you hold a clean title, not to see the document.

What buyers readThat the maintenance story has paper behind it.

Things that quietly cost you money: shooting in a cluttered driveway with a trash can in frame; your reflection in the door; portrait orientation so the car is small in a tall picture; a heavy filter; night shots under a streetlight; and the single worst one — only four photographs. A fifteen-frame listing at a fair price outperforms a four-frame listing at a lower one, because the low price on a thin listing reads as a warning rather than a bargain.

This is our own shot list from photographing inventory on a small lot. Nothing here is measured or surveyed data about buyer behavior — it is what we have found works, offered as a starting point you should adapt to your car.
