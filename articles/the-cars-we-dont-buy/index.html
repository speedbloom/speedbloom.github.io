Condition

The cars we don't buy

Six cars out front is the visible end of a much longer list, and the invisible part is where the standards actually live. This is the catalog we work from in an auction row with a flashlight and four minutes — each criterion, the reason for it, and the exception where one honestly exists.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

None of these are moral judgments about the cars. A branded-title car is a legitimate vehicle that somebody should own; a modified car is somebody's pride. The list is about what a six-space lot can buy, inspect, describe accurately and stand behind. The criteria are standing rules applied across many sales, and where an example appears it is representative rather than a report on one particular car.

Title and paperwork 01–03

The fastest rejections, because they require no mechanical judgment at all.

01

Branded titles — salvage, rebuilt, flood, lemon-law buyback, junk

The brand follows the car permanently, affects what insurers and future lenders will do with it, and narrows the buyer pool for every owner afterward. A rebuilt car can be well repaired and still be a car we can't price honestly against six unbranded ones on the same lot.

The honest version: a business limitation, not a verdict. Dealers who specialize in branded vehicles and price them correctly do legitimate work. We aren't set up for it.

02

A title that isn't clean, isn't present, or isn't explainable

Missing lien releases, an assignment chain with gaps, a seller who cannot say where the document is or when it will arrive. Each can be innocent and each can take months, during which the car cannot legally be sold.

The exception: a title in transit from a lienholder with a payoff confirmation and a date. That is ordinary. "It's coming" with no paperwork behind it is not.

03

Odometer trouble of any kind

A discrepancy in the history, a "true mileage unknown" designation, a cluster replaced without documentation, or wear that argues with the number — a polished steering wheel on a car claiming low miles.

No exception. Mileage is the one figure the entire price is built on. If it can't be trusted, nothing downstream can.

Structure and history 04–07

Where the paint gauge, the flashlight and the trunk trim earn their keep.

04

Announced structural or frame damage

A properly repaired structural car can drive perfectly well. It also carries a disclosure that follows it to every subsequent sale, and verifying the quality of the original repair requires equipment and records we usually don't have access to at the point of purchase.

The exception: none at auction speed. The judgment required is beyond what ninety seconds and a condition report supports.

05

Evidence of repair with no record of it

Paint depth that jumps on one panel, seam sealer laid by a finger instead of a machine, overspray inside a fuel door, a bolt with rounded flats. Not a crime — cars get hit and cars get fixed. The disqualifier is the combination of visible repair and a documentary trail that says nothing happened, because then we cannot describe the car truthfully at any price. One version of that morning is written up in the car we sent to auction.

The exception: documented repair, with an invoice, to a non-structural panel. That is history, not mystery.

06

Airbag deployment without documented, correct replacement

Restraint systems are not a component but a system — modules, sensors, pretensioners, wiring, and a dash that may have been apart. A warning light made to go away without the hardware behind it being right is a serious failure mode, and not always visible.

No exception without full repair documentation and a scan that agrees with it.

07

Flood indicators

Silt in the seat rails or spare well, a tide line in the trunk, corrosion on connector pins, carpet that doesn't match its padding, a dehumidified smell over an organic one. Water damage expresses itself electrically, months later, in ways no inspection can forecast.

No exception, branded or not. Plenty of flood cars carry clean titles from states where nobody filed a claim.

Mechanical 08–11

Judgment calls made in a parking row, with the engine running and the windows down.

08

A transmission that does anything unusual

A flare between gears, a clunk on the roll, hunting at low speed, a delay engaging reverse. A correct repair on a modern automatic can cost more than the whole margin on the car, and the distance between "needs a fluid service" and "needs a unit" cannot be established from a parking lot.

No exception. This is the single most expensive way to be optimistic in this business.

09

Signs of overheating or oil-coolant intermix

Residue in the coolant reservoir, an emulsion under the oil cap, a replaced radiator on an otherwise untouched car. The cooling system is what kills engines, and the damage from one past event is frequently invisible until the day it isn't.

The exception: documented cooling work with an explanation that fits what's in front of you — a replaced hose on a ten-year-old car is maintenance, not history.

10

A noise on cold start that nobody has diagnosed

Chain rattle for the first seconds, a tick that fades, a whine that changes with load. Some of those are benign characteristics of a particular engine and some are the opening move of a very large bill, and the only way to know is teardown or history — neither available in the row.

The exception: a documented, known characteristic backed by service records showing it was investigated.

11

Missing or modified emissions hardware

Absent catalytic converters, deleted diesel aftertreatment, tuner software on a car that has to be inspected. Beyond the legal question, restoring this equipment correctly is frequently a four-figure job, and the car cannot be sold to an ordinary buyer until it is.

No exception. Requirements are set by state and federal rules, and confirming them for your situation is the state's job to answer, not ours.

Economics and fit 12–15

Cars with nothing wrong with them that are still wrong for a lot this size.

12

Parts we can't get quickly

Low-volume models, discontinued trims, anything where a common component is dealer-only and back-ordered. A car waiting three weeks on a part occupies a lift, a space and a line of credit.

The exception: a well-supported model with one unusual part we've already confirmed is available before bidding.

13

Modified cars

Lowered or lifted suspension, forced induction added after the fact, a tune, track use. We can't verify who did the work or how, we can't warrant it, and the pool of buyers for somebody else's project is small.

The exception: bolt-on cosmetics that can be returned to stock, and a set of original parts in the trunk.

14

A car that only works at a price nobody is paying

The one that catches everyone: the car is fine and the bidding has gone past where an honest retail number leaves room. The pull to buy anyway is strong, because empty spaces earn nothing and the day feels wasted. That pull is the enemy.

The discipline: the number is set before the bidding starts, in writing, and it doesn't move because the room got competitive.

15

Anything we can't explain

The catch-all, and the one that has saved us the most money. A car priced well below where it should be has a reason, and the reason is always somewhere. If we can't find it and can't account for it, the correct conclusion is not that we got lucky.

The rule: if you can't say out loud why it's cheap, it isn't cheap.

What this is good for if you're shopping

Use it as a question rather than a checklist. Ask a dealer — us included — what they turned down recently and why. Specifics (a panel, a reading, a noise, a title that hadn't arrived) describe a business with standards. Adjectives describe one without them.

And notice how much of this you can check yourself. Wear against mileage, silt in the seat rails, a transmission that hesitates into reverse, a paint gauge on the quarter panels — none of it requires a license. Most of the list above is just looking, in an order, by someone who decided in advance what would make them walk away.

This catalog describes our own buying standards in general terms. Items and examples are representative rather than reports on particular vehicles, and no auction, seller or dealership is characterized. Title-branding rules, emissions requirements and inspection standards are set by state and federal authorities and change; confirm anything that affects a purchase with the relevant agency.
