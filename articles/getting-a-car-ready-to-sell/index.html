Selling

Getting a car ready to sell

Ranked strictly by what you get back for the effort, which is not the same order most people do things in. The top of this list costs almost nothing. The bottom of it is where sellers spend money they never recover.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

We recondition cars for a living, so we have a fairly unsentimental view of which jobs move a car and which ones just feel productive. The order below is the order we would do them in on our own inventory, and it is deliberately front-loaded: if you only do the first three, you have captured most of what is available to capture.

1

Clean the interior like you mean it

Cost
Low
Your time
Half a day
Returns
More than anything else here

Nothing on this list is close. A buyer decides how a car was treated within about four seconds of opening the door, and almost all of that judgment is formed by the cabin, not the paint. Vacuum under the seats and in the seat tracks. Wipe the door jambs — nobody does the jambs, and a clean one reads as care. Clean the inside of the glass, both windshields, properly; every used car in the world has a film on the inside of the windshield and it makes the whole car feel tired.

Then deal with the smell. Smoke and dog are the two that cost real money, and neither is fixed with an air freshener hung from the mirror — that reads as concealment and buyers know it. Pull the mats, clean them separately, replace the cabin air filter, and air the car out for a day.

2

Put the paperwork in one folder

Cost
Free
Your time
An hour
Returns
Shortens the negotiation

The title, in your hand, in your name, with nothing crossed out. Every service receipt you can find, even the ones from the quick-lube place. Both keys — a missing second key is a real deduction, because replacing a modern key with its programming is not a small bill. The owner's manual if it still exists.

This is free and it does more for your price than a hundred dollars of wax. A folder of records converts your claim that the car was maintained into something the buyer can verify, and a buyer who can verify things stops discounting for what they cannot see.

3

Resolve every warning light — or explain it in writing

Cost
Varies, sometimes none
Your time
One shop visit
Returns
Removes the worst unknown

An illuminated check engine light is not a small deduction, it is a negotiating cliff — because the buyer has to price the worst thing it could possibly be. Get it read. If it is a cheap sensor, fix it. If it is expensive, you now have a choice to make with real information instead of dread, and either way you can say exactly what it is.

Disconnecting the battery to clear the light is the worst thing you can do here. It comes back, usually on the test drive, and now you look like someone who hid it.

4

The small cheap stuff, all of it

Cost
Low
Your time
An afternoon
Returns
Removes free ammunition

Burned-out bulbs. Wiper blades that chatter. A cracked washer nozzle that sprays sideways. The trim clip that lets a piece of the door card stand proud. A glovebox light. Correct tire pressures. None of these is worth money on its own. Collectively they are the list a buyer recites back to you while asking for four hundred dollars off, and each one you fix is one they cannot say.

5

Photograph it properly

Cost
Free
Your time
Forty minutes
Returns
Decides whether anyone calls

This belongs higher in one sense — nothing else on the list matters if nobody clicks the listing — but it sits at five because it is downstream of the cleaning. Photographs of a dirty car are a waste of good light. Do the detail first, then shoot it. The shot list is here, including the frames buyers read the most into.

6

An oil change, dated and receipted

Cost
Low
Your time
An hour
Returns
Small, but it closes a question

Not because it changes what the car is worth, but because "oil changed last week, here's the receipt" is a sentence that ends a line of questioning instead of opening one. It also means the dipstick looks like what a careful owner's dipstick looks like, and people do check.

7

The exterior wash and a one-step polish

Cost
Low
Your time
Two or three hours
Returns
Real, but less than you'd guess

Wash, clay if the paint feels rough, one pass of a light polish, dress the tires. This makes the photographs better and the walk-up better. What it will not do is hide anything — sunlight finds every swirl — so treat it as presentation rather than repair.

8

Paintless dent removal, on the one dent people see

Cost
Moderate
Your time
One appointment
Returns
Only on a visible, single dent

One door ding on a driver's-side panel is worth removing because it is the thing the buyer's eye keeps returning to. Six scattered dings on a ten-year-old car are not, because at that point they read as normal wear and nobody is pricing them individually.

9

Replace tires only if they are actually done

Cost
High
Your time
One appointment
Returns
Rarely the full cost

If the tires are legally and visibly finished, replace them — a buyer discounts worn tires by more than a set costs, and an inspection failure kills a sale outright. If they have a season left, leave them, say so plainly in the listing, and let the buyer price it. Buying tires to get credit for tires is usually a donation.

Below the line

These are the jobs sellers most often do and most often regret. Each one can be right in an unusual case; none is right by default.

Body shop paint repair

A repainted panel is worth less to a careful buyer than an honestly disclosed scratch, because repaint raises the question of what else was repaired. Spend this money only on damage severe enough to stop someone from buying the car.

Major mechanical work on a car you're discounting anyway

If the car needs a big repair, you have two clean options: fix it and price it as a sound car, or disclose it and price it as a car that needs it. What loses money is half-fixing it and still having to disclose.

Steam-cleaning the engine bay

An immaculate engine bay on a high-mileage car reads as staged, not as maintained — and anyone experienced will wonder what leak was being washed away. Wipe the dust off. Stop there.

Aftermarket anything

New wheels, a stereo, tint, a spoiler. You are paying for something that narrows your buyer pool. The stock parts you took off are worth more to the sale than the parts you put on.

Deodorizing instead of cleaning

Odor eliminators buy you about twenty minutes. The smell comes back during the test drive, at which point the buyer knows both that the car smells and that you tried to cover it.

The whole list in one sentence. Spend your money on removing the buyer's unknowns — smells, lights, missing keys, missing records — and almost none of it on making the car look newer than it is. Unknowns are what get priced harshly. Honest age does not.

Cost and time bands on this page are our own qualitative judgment from reconditioning cars on a small lot, not survey data or measured averages. What any individual job returns depends entirely on the car, its condition and the buyer in front of it.
