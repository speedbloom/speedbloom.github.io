Selling

Donating or selling an old car

There are five ways a tired car leaves a driveway and people usually pick between two of them. Donation is a genuinely good answer to one specific situation and a quietly poor answer to a different one, and the line between them is not where most articles put it.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

First, the thing we are not going to do. We are not going to tell you anything about the tax treatment of a donated vehicle — not a figure, not a threshold, not a rule of thumb. Those rules exist, they have changed over the years, they depend on facts about you, and an article's summary is exactly the wrong basis for a tax position. If a deduction is part of your reasoning, that conversation belongs with a tax professional or with the IRS's own published guidance on vehicle donations, before you sign anything.

What follows is everything else: what each exit actually gets you, what it costs you, and what happens to the car afterward.

1. Private sale, honestly described

Listed as what it is, priced accordingly.

What you get
The most money of any route, by a distance, even on a rough car. There is a real market for cheap running vehicles and for cars sold as projects, and it is bigger than people assume.
What it costs you
A listing, photographs, messages, and meetings — and on a low-value car the buyers can be harder work per dollar than on a good one. Budget a few weekends.
What happens to the car
Someone drives it, or someone takes the parts off it. Either way it gets used, which is the environmentally better outcome and the one people tend not to count.
The catch
You must disclose what is wrong with it, in writing, and price it as a car with those problems. Selling a failing car as a good one to a private buyer is how people end up in arguments they deserve to lose.
Best whenThe car still runs, or it is a common model whose parts people want, and you have a few weekends.

2. Sell or trade it to a dealer

Including as a trade against something else.

What you get
Less than private sale, more than scrap — if the car is something a dealer can retail or wholesale. Below a certain condition, many dealers will simply decline, and a polite decline is not an insult.
What it costs you
An afternoon. The paperwork, the payoff if there is one, and the title handling all become somebody else's problem.
What happens to the car
Reconditioned and retailed, or sent to a dealer auction and bought by someone who specializes in exactly this. We have written about that path.
The catch
On a genuinely tired car the offer may be close to scrap money, because that is close to what it is worth to us. Ask what it would take to make it retailable; the answer explains the number.
Best whenYou are buying something else anyway, or you want it handled in one appointment with the paperwork done correctly.

3. An auto recycler or scrap yard

The end of the line, done properly.

What you get
A modest sum, usually weight-based with some allowance for usable parts, paid on collection. Call three yards — quotes on the same car vary more than you would expect.
What it costs you
One phone call and being home when the truck arrives. Most yards tow it free; confirm that before agreeing, because a tow charge can consume the whole payment.
What happens to the car
Parts are pulled and resold, fluids are handled, the shell is crushed and recycled. A licensed recycler does this to a standard; an unlicensed one may not.
The catch
Use a licensed operation, get a receipt naming the vehicle and the VIN, take your plates off, and follow the New Jersey MVC's current procedure for disposing of a vehicle and surrendering plates. Never hand over a car with the title left blank.
Best whenThe car does not run, is not worth repairing, and nobody wants it whole.

4. Donate it to a charity

The one people ask about, and the one with the most moving parts.

What you get
The car gone, usually with free towing, usually within a few days, with no listing and no strangers. Whatever tax consequence follows is a separate question for a professional — see the top of this page.
What it costs you
The proceeds. Whatever the car would have sold for privately, you are choosing not to receive.
What happens to the car
This is the part worth understanding. A few charities use donated vehicles directly or give them to families. Many programs instead sell the car and pass a share of the proceeds to the charity, often through a third-party processor that handles collection, titling and sale and is paid out of the same money. The share reaching the charity varies considerably between programs.
The catch
If the car has real resale value, selling it and donating the cash almost always delivers more money to the cause than donating the car does — because you are not paying for a tow, a title transfer and an auction out of the same pot. That is the honest comparison, and it is the one the donation programs do not make for you.
Best whenThe car is hard to sell, you want it gone without effort, and you have confirmed how the specific program works and who receives what.

5. Give it to someone who needs it

Underrated, and the only route where you see the result.

What you get
Nothing financially. A car that would have brought scrap money instead becomes somebody's transportation to work.
What it costs you
The proceeds, plus a real obligation to be honest. Handing a friend or a relative a car with a known serious fault, unexplained, is a favor that turns into a grievance.
What happens to the car
It keeps working, which is the best outcome available for a vehicle that still has life in it.
The catch
Transfer the title properly, take your plates, and do not leave the registration or insurance in your name as a convenience. Every informal arrangement we have seen go wrong went wrong at exactly that point.
Best whenThe car runs, someone you know needs one, and you would rather see it used than banked.

If you are leaning toward donating

Donating is not a worse choice than selling — it is a different transaction, and the reason to do it is that you want a cause to benefit, not that it is the efficient way to dispose of a car. Treat it with the same care you would give a sale.

Five questions to ask the charity before the truck arrives

  1. Does the organization run this program itself, or through a processor? Both are legitimate; the answer changes the arithmetic.
  2. What share of the sale proceeds does the charity actually receive? A well-run program will answer this plainly.
  3. What will happen to this specific car — used, given away, or sold?
  4. What documentation will I receive, and when? Get the answer in writing, and give it to whoever prepares your taxes rather than interpreting it yourself.
  5. Who handles the title and the plates, and what do I need to file? Confirm the New Jersey side against the MVC's current instructions, not against the tow driver's summary.

The rule that covers all five routes. The car stops being your problem on the day its title is properly transferred out of your name and your plates are off it — not on the day it leaves the driveway. Signed title, completed odometer disclosure, plates removed, whatever notice the MVC requires filed, insurance cancelled only once the transfer is real. Every horror story in this category is a story about a car that physically left but legally stayed.

Nothing on this page is tax advice, and we have deliberately stated no rule, figure or threshold concerning charitable deductions for donated vehicles — consult a tax professional or the IRS's current guidance. Titling, plate surrender and disposal requirements are set by the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission and change from time to time; confirm them with the MVC. Descriptions of how donation programs are structured are general; ask the specific organization about its own arrangement.
