Buying

Buying for a growing family

The change isn't "we need something bigger." It's a specific set of geometric and logistical facts, nearly all of which can be established in a parking lot in twenty minutes with a tape measure and the things you already own.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

Families shop for cars the way everyone else does — by class, by brand, by which one looked right in the photographs — and then discover afterward that the trunk won't take the stroller, or that the doors don't open far enough in a normal parking space to get a child into a seat. These are measurable things, and measuring them is faster than reading reviews about them.

So this piece is a worksheet rather than an argument. Print it, or copy the fields onto your phone, and fill it in per car. It will disagree with your shortlist, which is the point.

What we're not going to tell you

We are not going to tell you which car seats fit which cars, how many anchors a vehicle has, or whether three seats will go across a particular back row. Not because it isn't important — it's the most important thing on this page — but because the answer is set by two documents that are specific to your seat and your car: the car seat manufacturer's instructions and the vehicle's owner manual. Both address which seating positions may be used, in what way, and with what hardware, and both are authoritative in a way that a dealer's summary is not.

Read those two, and if you want a second pair of hands, certified child passenger safety technicians do installation checks — many police departments, fire departments and hospitals in New Jersey host them. That's the right source. We sell cars.

What we can do is tell you what to measure so that you arrive at those documents with the right car already in front of you.

Bring to the lot

  • A tape measure.
  • The actual car seat you own or intend to buy — installed, not held up against the bench.
  • The stroller, folded the way you fold it in real life.
  • The person who is tallest, and the person who does most of the buckling.
  • The vehicle's owner manual, if it's in the glovebox. If it isn't, ask whether one can be found.

Two things worth thinking about before you shop

Don't buy for four years from now unless it's cheap to do so. People move up a whole vehicle class for a hypothetical third child, and then drive a larger, thirstier car for years while the hypothetical remains hypothetical. If the bigger car costs little more to own, fine. If it costs meaningfully more in fuel, tires and insurance, you are paying now for a problem you may not have — and cars can be sold when the situation changes.

Third rows deserve a specific test, not a general one. The question isn't whether a third row exists. It's whether you can use it and still carry what you need to carry on the same trip. Fold it up, put the stroller behind it, and see. A third row that eliminates the cargo space is a third row you'll use twice a year.

What changes less than people expect

Engine output, drivetrain and trim level rarely change family life. All-wheel drive is a New Jersey-winter conversation rather than a children conversation, and it has its own costs. A larger engine mostly buys you a fuel bill. Meanwhile the door aperture, the trunk opening and the width of the back bench will affect every single day you own the car, and none of them appear in a single review headline.

Which is the whole argument for the worksheet. Measure the things that are measurable, read the two manuals for the things that aren't, and let the shortlist lose to the tape measure.

This page contains no claims about child seat fitment, anchor systems or seating position suitability. Those are determined by your car seat manufacturer's instructions and by your vehicle's owner manual, both of which are specific to the products involved and take precedence over anything here. Free installation checks by certified child passenger safety technicians are available at many New Jersey fire departments, police departments and hospitals.
