Buying

When high mileage is fine

Mileage is not a grade out of a hundred. It is a band, and inside each band exactly one thing governs whether the number on the odometer tells you anything useful. Here are the five bands and what rules each of them.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

Buyers treat mileage as a single continuous scale where lower is always better, and dealers price it roughly that way because the market does. But the reason mileage matters changes as the number climbs. Under sixty thousand it is mostly a proxy for price. Past a hundred and fifty thousand it stops describing the car at all and starts describing its records. The bands below are market bands, not engineering thresholds — they are where buyer behavior and pricing shift, which is what you are actually navigating.

One framing before the bands: the useful comparison is never mileage alone but miles per year against how they were accumulated. Ninety thousand miles of highway commuting, one driver, one climate, is a gentler life than forty thousand miles of short winter trips that never bring the engine fully up to temperature.

0 – 60,000 miles
What governsPrice, not condition

In this band almost every car is mechanically boring, and that is the point. Wear items are largely original or on their first replacement, and the spread between a well-kept example and a neglected one is small enough that it rarely shows on a test drive. What you are really buying here is other people's depreciation.

Which means the analysis is financial, not mechanical. The premium over the next band down is often steep, and it buys you fewer years of trouble-free use than the price difference implies, because the next band is also mostly trouble-free. This is the band where paying attention to price discipline pays better than paying attention to the car.

  • What to check anyway
  • Tire date codes — a low-mileage car can still be riding on old rubber that has aged rather than worn.
  • Whether the miles are low because it sat. Cars that sit develop different problems than cars that drive.
  • The price of the same model one band up. If the gap is small, the gap is the whole decision.
60,000 – 90,000 miles
What governsWho did the maintenance

This is the first band where two identical cars can be genuinely different cars. Fluids have come due, brakes have come due, suspension bushings have started to age, and every one of those was either handled or postponed. The car does not usually announce which.

It is also the sweet spot most careful buyers end up in, because the steepest depreciation is behind it and the expensive wear is still mostly ahead. The deciding evidence is paperwork. A folder of receipts from one shop is worth more in this band than a clean history report, because the report records events and the receipts record habits.

  • What to check
  • Service records with dates and mileages, not just a claim that it was serviced.
  • Fluid condition by eye — transmission fluid and coolant tell you about the previous owner's attention span.
  • Whether brakes and tires were done together or the cheap half was done alone.
  • Any deferred item the seller names voluntarily. The ones who name them usually did the rest.
90,000 – 120,000 miles
What governsThe big-ticket items, and whether they're behind you

Here the question stops being "was it maintained" and becomes "which of the expensive jobs has already been paid for." Every engine family has its own list, and it is specific to that engine rather than to the mileage. If the engine uses a timing belt rather than a chain, there is a manufacturer-specified replacement interval — look it up for that exact engine and model year rather than assuming a round number, and treat a documented replacement as real money already spent on your behalf.

A car at 110,000 miles with recent receipts for the jobs that particular engine is known to need can be a better buy than the same model at 75,000 with no history. That is not a contrarian position; it is just accounting. The work is either done or it is in your future.

  • What to check
  • Model-specific known issues — search the exact engine and transmission, not the model name.
  • Receipts for any belt, water pump, or major fluid service, with the mileage at which it was done.
  • Cold start behavior, and whether anything settles down after thirty seconds that shouldn't.
  • An independent inspection on a lift. In this band it is no longer optional.
120,000 – 150,000 miles
What governsModel choice

Above roughly this line, the make and model matter more than the individual car. Some platforms are ordinary to own at this mileage — parts are cheap, any shop will touch them, and the failure modes are known and survivable. Others become specialist propositions where a single repair can exceed the purchase price.

The test is not reputation but economics: what does a common repair cost on this specific car, and who near you will do it? A model where the answer is "four hundred dollars, anybody" is a different ownership experience from one where the answer is "two thousand, and only the dealer has the tool."

  • What to check
  • Price a hypothetical repair before you buy — call a local independent shop and ask what a common job costs on that model.
  • Parts availability. Discontinued trim and electronics are the quiet killer at this mileage.
  • Rust, properly, from underneath. In New Jersey this is the band where salt exposure starts deciding a car's remaining life.
150,000 and up
What governsEvidence — or nothing at all

At this point the odometer has stopped being informative. A documented high-mileage car with a coherent history is a known quantity; an undocumented one is a lottery ticket, and the two can sit on the same lot at the same price. There is no inspection thorough enough to substitute for records here, because what you need to know is not the car's current state but its trajectory.

Bought correctly — cheaply, with evidence, with a mechanic's blessing, and with a realistic reserve for the first repair — these can be genuinely good transportation. Bought on hope, they are the most expensive cars on any lot. The deciding factor is whether the seller can show you the life it has had.

  • What to check
  • Continuous records. Gaps of several years matter more than any single entry.
  • Whether you could absorb a significant repair in month three without it becoming a crisis.
  • Frame, subframe and mounting points — the structural stuff salt reaches first.
  • The seller's answer to "what would you fix next if you were keeping it?"

The band you are in changes the question. Low miles: is the price right? Middle miles: who looked after it? High miles: can you see the proof? Asking the high-mileage question about a low-mileage car wastes an afternoon, and asking the low-mileage question about a high-mileage one is how people buy the wrong car cheaply.

The mileage bands above describe how the used market prices and discusses cars, not any manufacturer's engineering specification. Service and replacement intervals are set by the manufacturer for each specific engine and model year — confirm them in the owner's manual or with a qualified shop rather than from any general figure.
