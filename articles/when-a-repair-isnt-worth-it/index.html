When a repair isn't worth it

"Never spend more on a repair than the car is worth" is the rule everybody quotes, and it is the wrong arithmetic. The car's value is not what you are buying with the repair. What you are buying is months.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 4 min read

The reason the usual rule fails is that it compares a repair to the wrong thing. You are not choosing between spending $3,000 and having $3,000. You are choosing between spending $3,000 and doing something else that also costs money — because the alternative to a repaired car is not no car.

So the useful number is cost per remaining month, on both sides. Five inputs, one division, and then a comparison.

The baseline we'll compare against

All four cases below get measured against the same illustrative replacement: a $12,000 used car, kept four years, plus about $1,000 of sales tax, title, registration and fees. That is $13,000 over 48 months, or roughly $271 a month — before one drop of fuel, one insurance payment or one oil change. Your own version of this number is the one that matters; ours is here to make the shape of the comparison visible.

Case one

The big repair on the old car

Transmission, on a car you have owned for years and that has otherwise been honest with you.

A The quote, in writing$3,400
B Other work due within a year$900 Tires and rear brakes. Ask the shop what else they saw while it was up.
C Worth repaired / worth as it sits$4,500 / $900
D Months of service you honestly expect24
E Total spend ÷ months$4,300 ÷ 24
Cost per month$179

Repair. $179 against $271, and the gap is wide enough to survive being wrong about the 24 months. Note what the old rule would have said: $3,400 on a $4,500 car, don't do it.

Case two

The same size repair, on a car with a ceiling

Engine work quoted at $2,600. The car also has rust through the rocker panels and an inspection coming.

A The quote, in writing$2,600
B Other work due within a year$600
C Worth repaired / worth as it sits$3,000 / $600
D Months of service you honestly expect12 This is the line that decides the case. Structural corrosion is a hard ceiling — it is not repaired by fixing the engine, and it does not get better.
E Total spend ÷ months$3,200 ÷ 12
Cost per month$267

Don't. Nearly the same monthly number as the replacement — but it buys twelve months instead of forty-eight, and at the end of it you are standing here again with a car worth less. The repair purchases a delay, not remaining life.

Case three

The four-figure quote that frightens people unnecessarily

$1,900 of suspension and brake work on a much newer car with a clean history and no other work outstanding.

A The quote, in writing$1,900
B Other work due within a year$0
C Worth repaired / worth as it sits$16,000 / $13,500
D Months of service you honestly expect60
E Total spend ÷ months$1,900 ÷ 60
Cost per month$32

Repair, without hesitating. Included because people panic at the size of a quote regardless of what sits underneath it. A four-figure number on a car with five years left in it is a rounding error.

Case four

The one the arithmetic doesn't settle

$2,200 of work. The car has also left you on the shoulder twice in eight months, and you drive the Parkway at six in the morning.

A The quote, in writing$2,200
B Other work due within a year$0
C Worth repaired / worth as it sits$6,000 / $2,400
D Months of service you honestly expect24
E Total spend ÷ months$2,200 ÷ 24
Cost per month$92

Your call, and money isn't the reason. The arithmetic says repair by a wide margin. Two roadside failures say something the worksheet cannot hold. Replacing the car here is a defensible choice — just make it in the right words: "I am paying about $180 a month extra for reliability I can count on," not "the repair wasn't worth it."

Five ways this goes wrong

1. Counting what you already spent. "I just put $1,200 into it in June" is the most common sentence in this conversation and it belongs in none of the five lines. That money is gone either way. It is not an argument for spending more and it is not an argument against.

2. Guessing at line D with your feelings. Months of remaining service is the number the whole thing pivots on, and it is the one you are least equipped to estimate. Pressure-test it by asking your shop a direct question: if I do this repair, what is the next thing likely to need money?

3. Forgetting the "as it sits" value. A car with a dead transmission is not worth zero. Get a real number for it before you decide, because it is the down payment on the alternative — and because the difference between repaired value and as-is value tells you what the repair creates.

4. Pricing the alternative as a car, not a transaction. Sales tax, title, registration, documentary fee, the first insurance change, and your own hours. All of it belongs in the replacement's column.

5. Accepting one quote. On anything large, get a second. Line A is the input most likely to move, and a second opinion sometimes changes the diagnosis rather than just the price.
