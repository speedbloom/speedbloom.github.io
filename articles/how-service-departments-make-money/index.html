Owning

How service departments make money

Almost everything that feels odd about a repair bill follows from one structural fact: labor is sold by the book hour and not the clock hour. Here is the map of incentives that grows out of that — and the opposite incentive that runs through our own bay, which we have no business leaving out of it.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

None of what follows is an accusation. Incentives are not corruption; they are the shape of the ground water runs down. A shop with these incentives and a conscience produces good work and a fair bill most of the time. A shop with these incentives and no one asking questions drifts, slowly, in a direction that is entirely predictable. Knowing the structure is how you tell which one you're in.

Compensation and billing practices differ from shop to shop; what follows is common industry structure rather than a description of any particular business. The way to know how your shop works is to ask, and a good one will tell you.

The map

The book hour

Labor is usually billed from a published time guide: this job on this vehicle is allotted, say, 2.4 hours, and you are billed 2.4 hours at the shop's labor rate whether the technician takes ninety minutes or four. The estimate you approve is built from those times.

Which means: speed pays. A technician who beats the book on a job they have done fifty times is genuinely, legitimately earning more per clock hour — and the same math quietly punishes the careful job on an unfamiliar car.

Flagged hours

In many shops the technician is paid on those same book hours — hours "flagged" to jobs — rather than on time at work. A bay with no car in it pays nothing.

Which means: a technician's income depends on volume of billable work arriving, which is a pressure that exists before anyone looks at your car. It is also why good technicians leave shops that are slow, and why a busy shop can be a good sign.

Diagnosis

An intermittent fault can eat an afternoon and flag almost nothing. The book has a time for replacing a part; it has a much less generous relationship with finding out which part.

Which means: the structure rewards replacing and penalizes investigating. This is the honest origin of parts-cannon repairs — not malice, just a pay plan that pays for the second thing and not the first. Shops that charge a real, separate diagnostic fee are usually the ones that will actually diagnose.

Parts

Parts are sold at a markup over cost, on a scale that typically rises as the part gets cheaper. Labor and parts are two different profit centers on the same invoice.

Which means: a job that is parts-heavy and labor-light is a good job for the shop. It also means the choice between original, aftermarket and remanufactured parts is a margin decision as well as a quality one, and you are entitled to be part of it.

The advisor

The person at the counter is frequently compensated on the department's gross or on hours sold, not on a salary alone. They are the interface between a technician's findings and your decision.

Which means: "while we're in there" is a sentence with economics behind it. Sometimes it is also completely correct — bundling work that shares labor genuinely saves you money. Both things are true at once, which is what makes it hard.

The inspection sheet

The multi-point check with its green, yellow and red columns is a real safety instrument and the department's primary sales instrument, and it is both simultaneously. Nothing on it is usually invented. What is editorial is the column a finding lands in.

Which means: the useful question is not "is this true?" but "what happens if I wait?" — and then asking for the measurement. Brake pads at four millimeters and brake pads at two millimeters are both "yellow" at some shops.

Menus and intervals

Some recommended services come from the manufacturer's published maintenance schedule. Others are the shop's own package, assembled for mileage milestones and priced as a bundle.

Which means: ask which one you're being offered. "Is this in the maintenance schedule in my owner's manual, or is it your shop's recommendation?" is a neutral question with a checkable answer, and your manual is the authority — not us, and not an article.

Declined work

Everything you say no to is recorded, and the shop's system will surface it the next time you appear, and often in a reminder before that.

Which means: declining is not a fight, it's a data entry. Get the finding written on the invoice with its measurement, and you own a dated record of the car's condition — genuinely useful when you sell it.

Now the part about us

We have a service bay. It would be convenient to describe it as pure virtue and leave the incentive discussion aimed at everybody else. The truth is that our bay runs on an incentive just as strong as the ones above — it simply points the other way, and that is not automatically better.

Most of what our lift does is reconditioning our own inventory. Every hour and every part spent on a car we own comes directly out of that car's gross profit, and the person deciding whether the work is necessary is the person whose money it is. A customer-pay shop has a pull toward finding more. A dealer's recon bay has a pull toward finding less — toward "it'll pass," toward one tire instead of two, toward calling a weep a seep.

That pull is real and we feel it on specific cars. The structural guard we use against it is publication: the 168-point inspection is written as the car is found, and published whole, including the items we chose not to fix and why. Declining work in private is free. Declining it on a page anyone can read next to the price is expensive enough to keep us honest — and when it doesn't, the item is right there in writing for a buyer, or their own mechanic, to point at.

We also do customer-pay work, mostly for people who bought cars here. On that side, the arithmetic at the top of this page is our arithmetic too. We are not claiming to have escaped it. We are saying that a business ought to be able to describe its own incentives out loud, and that a shop which cannot is telling you something.

What to actually do at the counter

  • Sort the list. "Which of these is safety, which is function, and which is maintenance?" Three categories, three different urgencies, one invoice that usually mixes them.
  • Ask what happens if you wait. A real answer sounds like "the pad has about this much left and you'll hear it before it damages the rotor." An unreal answer is a tone of voice.
  • Ask for the measurement, not the color. Millimeters, thirty-seconds, pounds of pressure, a code number. Written on the invoice.
  • Ask for the book time and the labor rate separately. They are two different numbers and you are allowed to see both.
  • Ask for the old parts back when the job is significant. Most shops will do it without comment. Watching the request land is informative.
  • Set your own second-opinion threshold — a dollar figure above which you always get another look, decided in advance while you are calm rather than in a waiting room while your car is on a lift.
  • Never authorize by phone without a number. "Go ahead and take care of it" is the single most expensive sentence a car owner says.

The test of a good shop. Not that it never recommends anything — a shop that finds nothing on a ten-year-old car isn't looking. It's whether the recommendations come with measurements, whether declining one is uneventful, and whether the same items reappear next visit with the numbers moving in a direction that makes sense.

Pay plans, labor guides, parts matrices and warranty reimbursement practices vary by shop, by brand and by region; the structures described here are common ones, not universal rules, and nothing here states any particular manufacturer's, insurer's or shop's policy. Maintenance requirements for your vehicle are the ones in your owner's manual.
