Owning

Records that hold value

Most people keep either nothing or everything, and a glovebox stuffed with undated register receipts is closer to nothing than its owner thinks. What holds value is a folder with seven dividers in it — and one page on top that nobody ever makes.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

We appraise other people's cars most weeks, which means we spend a fair amount of time being handed paperwork. The pattern is consistent enough to state plainly: a record is only worth something if it carries a date, a mileage and a name. A receipt with a total and nothing else proves that money was spent somewhere. That is not the same as proving anything about your car.

Here is the folder, divider by divider.

1 · Ownership

Title, registration, lien release

The title, the current registration, and — if you financed the car — the lien release or the letter confirming the loan was satisfied. If the lien was cleared but the title still shows it, resolve that now rather than on the day a buyer is standing in your driveway with cash.

The title does not live in the car. It lives at home. A title in a glovebox is a stolen car's paperwork waiting to happen, and replacing one is a trip and a fee you did not need.

Without this divider there is no sale at all. Everything else on this page is about price; this one is about whether a transaction can legally occur.
2 · The purchase

How the car became yours

  • The bill of sale and the buyer's guide
  • The odometer disclosure
  • Any written promise the seller made about work to be done
  • The inspection report the car came with, if it came with one
  • Financing documents, if there are any

This divider establishes the start line: what the car was, what it read, and what was disclosed on the day you took it.

Lets the next buyer see the car's condition at your starting point, which makes everything you did afterward legible as improvement rather than mystery.
3 · Service

Every invoice, with mileage on it

Shop invoices, itemized, on letterhead, each one showing the date and the odometer. If a shop hands you a receipt without the mileage, ask them to add it — it takes four seconds and it is the entire difference between a document and a scrap.

Do-it-yourself work counts too, if you record it the same way: date, mileage, what was done, and the parts receipt stapled to it.

This is the divider a buyer flips through to answer one question — was this car looked after by someone, or only reacted to?
4 · Big tickets

The expensive things, kept separate

Pull these out of the general service pile and give them their own divider, because they are the items a buyer is mentally pricing while they walk around the car:

  • Tires — keep the invoice, because it dates them and a buyer can check it against the date codes on the sidewall
  • Brakes, battery, alternator, starter
  • Timing belt, water pump, clutch
  • Air conditioning work, catalytic converter, major suspension
These receipts do something no amount of general tidiness does: they take specific, expensive worries off the buyer's list one at a time.
5 · Damage

Keep the accident paperwork. Yes, really.

The instinct is to throw this away. It is the wrong instinct. If the car was hit and repaired, the event may well appear on a history report whether you kept the file or not — and at that point the only variable left is whether you can show what was done.

Keep the claim number, the estimate, and above all the itemized body shop invoice: which panels, which parts, replaced or repaired, refinished where.

A documented, properly repaired collision is a conversation. An undocumented one that surfaces on a report is a discount, because the buyer now has to price the worst version they can imagine.
6 · Official

Recalls, warranty work, state inspection

Dealer invoices showing recall work completed, any repairs done under a manufacturer's warranty or a service contract, and your New Jersey inspection records. Recall status for your VIN is also checkable directly through the manufacturer and through federal databases — worth doing once a year for free.

Answers the question a careful buyer asks and a casual one doesn't: is there anything outstanding on this car that somebody already agreed to fix?
7 · Hardware

The things that are not paper

  • Both keys — a missing second key is a real, quotable cost
  • The wheel lock key
  • The owner's manual and its wallet
  • The cargo cover, the factory floor mats, the roof rack hardware
  • The original stereo, if you replaced it
  • Day-one photographs of the car, dated by your phone
Cheap to keep, disproportionately expensive to replace, and visible in the first ninety seconds of any inspection.

The page nobody makes

One sheet, on top of the folder, four columns. Add a line each time anything is done. It takes twenty seconds and it converts a stack of paper into a story someone can read in a minute standing in a driveway.

DATE MILES WORK WHO 2024-03-11 41,208 Tires x4, alignment Ridgeway Tire 2024-09-02 48,760 Oil + filter, cabin filter self 2025-01-19 54,115 Front brakes, fluid flush Kelso Auto

So what is it worth?

We are not going to put a number on it, because nobody honestly can. What we can describe is the mechanism, from the side of the desk that makes the offer.

An appraisal is an exercise in pricing uncertainty. When we cannot tell whether a transmission has ever been serviced or whether those tires are two years old or seven, we do not split the difference — we assume the less convenient version, because we are the ones who have to stand behind the car afterward. Records do not make us pay a premium. They stop us from assuming the worst. That gap is where the money is, and on a private sale it shows up twice: in the price, and in how fast the car sells to the first serious person instead of the fifth.

Two honest limits. A folder does not fix a branded title, and it does not rescue a car that drives badly — the paperwork is a multiplier on the car's actual condition, not a substitute for it. And bulk is not the same as substance: forty undated gas-station receipts help nobody, while six properly dated invoices and one log sheet do the whole job.

Start today, not at the start. If you have owned the car for three years and kept nothing, you have not lost the opportunity — you have lost three years of it. Open the folder now. The log sheet can begin with today's date and today's mileage, and in two years it will be a two-year record.

Requirements for titles, registration and inspection in New Jersey are set by the state and change from time to time; confirm current rules with the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission rather than with this page.
