Paperwork

The title, explained

Everything else in a car deal is a negotiation. The title is a record. It is the one piece of paper that outlives the transaction, and four of its fields decide whether the sale can legally happen at all — so here is the whole document, field by field, with the callouts numbered.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

A certificate of title is not a receipt and it is not a registration. It is the state's statement of who owns a specific vehicle, printed on security paper and issued by the motor vehicle agency of whichever state last held the record. In New Jersey that is the Motor Vehicle Commission. Registration says the car may be driven on the road. Insurance says somebody will pay for the damage. The title says whose car it is — and only the title does that.

Below is a described specimen. Layouts differ from state to state and from one printing year to the next, so treat the arrangement as illustrative rather than as a picture of the document in your hand. The fields, though, are common to nearly every version, and the callouts are the ones worth reading carefully before anybody signs anything.

  1. Issuing state and control number

    Tells you which state's record system the car currently lives in, and gives that state a way to look this exact document up. A title issued by a state the seller has no connection to is not automatically a problem — cars move — but it is a reason to ask how the car got here.

  2. The VIN

    Read it against the car, not against the listing. The VIN is visible through the base of the windshield and repeated on a sticker or plate in the driver's door jamb. All three should agree, character for character. This is a thirty-second check that almost nobody performs.

  3. Odometer reading and status boxes

    The number is a snapshot from the last time the title was issued, not today's mileage. The boxes beside it matter more than the digits: a mark next to not actual or exceeds mechanical limits follows the car permanently and changes what it is worth.

  4. Brands and remarks

    Where salvage, rebuilt, flood and similar designations are printed. On a clean title this area is empty or says nothing of consequence. Brands are defined by each state individually and do not always survive a move across state lines intact, which is exactly why this field is worth reading rather than assuming.

  5. Owner names — and the word between them

    Two names joined by and generally means both people have to sign. Two names joined by or generally means either one can. People discover this at the kitchen table, with one signer present and the other at work. Check it while you are still making arrangements.

  6. Lienholder

    If a bank or credit union is printed here, that lender has a legal interest in the car. A title that still shows an unreleased lien cannot cleanly become yours until the loan is paid and the lienholder's release is recorded. This is the single most common reason a private sale stalls. We wrote a whole piece on how that unwinds — liens, and how they clear.

  7. Date of issue

    A title issued very recently on a car that has supposedly been in one family for years deserves a question. There are ordinary answers — a lien was paid off, a document was lost and replaced, the car changed states. There are also less ordinary ones.

  8. The seller's assignment

    The back of the document is where ownership actually moves. The seller signs exactly as their name is printed on the front. A signature that does not match the printed name is not a formality problem — it is the whole problem.

  9. The buyer's block and the transfer odometer

    Filled in completely, in ink, at the time of sale. A seller who signs and hands you a title with your side left blank has created what people on lots call an open title, and it is a bad idea for both of you. More on why in the odometer statement.

  10. Dealer reassignment

    A separate block that licensed dealers use to pass a car along without titling it in the dealership's own name. Seeing one used is normal. Seeing several used, on a car being sold to you by a person who says they are not a dealer, is worth pausing over.

The four fields that decide whether the sale can happen

Of everything above, four fields are not merely informative. If any one of them is wrong, there is no sale to be had at any price: the VIN must match the car, the owner name must match the person signing and their identification, the lienholder line must be clear or be cleared as part of the transaction, and the brand field must say what the seller told you it says. Everything else can be sorted out afterward. Those four cannot.

Timing, and why we will not quote you a number

New Jersey sets a window for transferring a title after a sale, and applies a penalty when it is missed. That window and that penalty have both changed over the years, and we are not going to print a number here that might be stale by the time you read it. Look it up on the MVC's own pages, or ask at an agency, before you plan your week around it. The same applies to the documents the MVC wants to see at the counter and what it charges: current, from the source, every time.

General information, not legal advice. This page describes how vehicle titles generally work and what their fields mean. It is not a legal opinion, it is not specific to your transaction, and title formats, requirements, deadlines and fees vary by state and change over time. Confirm anything that matters with the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission or the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs, and consult an attorney licensed in New Jersey before relying on any of this in a dispute.

How we handle it here

Six cars at a time means we see the title on every one of them before it ever reaches the front row, and we look at the same fields listed above, in the same order, for the same reasons. When you buy from us the title work is ours to complete and ours to get right. When you buy from a neighbor, it is yours — and thirty seconds with the document, in daylight, before any money moves, is the cheapest insurance in the entire transaction.
