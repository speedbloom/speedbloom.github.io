Buying

A first car for a teenager

The instincts are decent instincts — small, cheap, slow, something they'll like. Several of them are wrong in ways that cost money, and one of them is wrong in a way that matters more than money.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

A first car is the only car most people buy for somebody else's use and their own peace of mind, and those two goals pull apart more often than you'd expect. What follows is a framework, and most of it is an argument against things we hear on the lot every spring — instincts that sound responsible and lead somewhere else.

A note on method. We're reasoning from physics, from repair invoices and from what comes through the shop. Where a number would have helped, we didn't have one we could stand behind, so we didn't invent one. Crash performance is rated per model by organizations that actually test cars — look up the specific vehicle rather than trusting any dealer's summary, ours included.

The instinct

Get something small. It'll be cheap, and easier to handle.

Get something ordinary-sized, and spend the money on its mechanical condition.

Two claims are bundled in there. The handling one is fair: a car whose corners a new driver can place, with glass they can see out of, is easier to learn in. That argues for sightlines and a modest footprint — not for the smallest car on the lot.

The safety intuition runs the other way. When two vehicles collide, the lighter one undergoes the larger change in speed. That's conservation of momentum, not a study anyone needs to cite, and on roads full of crossovers and trucks it isn't a small effect. Small isn't disqualifying. It just shouldn't be the goal — it should be whatever the budget produces after the condition is right.

The instinct

Get something old and beat up. A dent won't matter on a first car.

The dents genuinely don't matter. The brakes, tires and suspension do, and "beat up" rarely stops at the paint.

Half of this is right, and it's the useful half: cosmetic damage makes a car cheaper for a reason that has nothing to do with how it drives. A scuffed bumper is one of the few honest discounts in the used market, and a first car is exactly where you should take it.

The trouble is that a car neglected cosmetically has usually been neglected elsewhere, and the parts that get skipped first are the ones a new driver has the least experience compensating for. Tires at the wear bars. Brakes that were "fine" two inspections ago. A wheel bearing that's been humming since spring. A $3,000 car carrying $2,200 of deferred work is a $5,200 car that spends its first month at a shop.

Tires are the place to spend. They're the whole interface between car and road, most often at the minimum on a cheap car, and a new driver is least able to feel the difference until it's needed.

The instinct

Get something slow so they can't speed.

Buy the stopping, the seeing and the warning. Speed isn't something you can engineer out at the dealership.

Every car on the road does highway speeds. The Parkway does not ask about horsepower. What an engine changes is how easy a bad half-second decision is to make — so no, don't buy the turbocharged version — but underpowered is one of the weakest levers available to you, and parents routinely spend the whole budget pulling it.

The strong levers are unglamorous. Tread depth. Brakes with life in them. Sightlines over the shoulder that don't require faith. And controls that can be operated without looking: a car that buries climate and volume in a touchscreen asks a new driver to take their eyes off the road to turn the heat down. Nobody mentions that one, and it's on our list every time.

The instinct

It's their first car. Make it something they'll be excited about.

Dull is a feature, and it's a feature you can explain out loud.

A car that's fun to drive is fun to drive quickly, and a car that draws attention tends to acquire an audience. Neither of those is a moral failing in a seventeen-year-old; they're just predictable, and the purchase is the moment you get to account for them.

There's a financial version of the same point. Body style, engine and model all feed into what a young driver costs to insure, and none of it appears on the window sticker. Get real quotes on two or three specific vehicles — by VIN, with the actual driver listed — before you buy. It changes more first-car decisions than anything a salesperson says.

The instinct

Stretch for the newest car you can manage. The safety technology is worth it.

Buy the newest car you can buy without stretching, then check what that specific car actually has.

This one is half right and the half matters. Newer cars generally carry more driver-assistance equipment, and structural design has moved over the years. But "generally" is doing heavy lifting: equipment varied by trim and option package, and two cars of the same model year can have completely different lists. Don't assume by year — look up how the specific VIN was built, find the window sticker if it still exists, and confirm what's there.

And the systems only help a driver who knows what they are. A backup camera, a blind-spot light and a lane-departure chime each need explaining once, deliberately, in an empty parking lot — not discovered at 65 mph. That's a twenty-minute job and almost nobody does it. Meanwhile the stretch itself is a risk: a budget pulled tight by the purchase is the budget that produces the deferred maintenance in the second instinct.

The instinct

Put the loan in their name. It'll teach them responsibility.

Decide what you're teaching, and don't teach it with a lien.

A loan on a first car creates an obligation that outlasts the reason for the car — the job that ends, the school that's out of state, the year they don't drive. It also pins the car in place: one you owe more on than it's worth can't be sold without bringing money to the table.

If the point is to teach what a car costs, the running costs already teach it in full. Insurance, fuel, a set of tires and the first unexpected $600 repair are a complete curriculum, and none of them leave a balance behind. If a co-signature is being discussed, read what that actually commits you to before anyone signs.

What we'd actually look for

The short list, in order

  • Tires with real tread, matched across the axle, not four different makers.
  • Brakes with life left and rotors that aren't at their minimum thickness.
  • A driving position the actual driver fits, and glass they can see out of.
  • Physical controls for climate and volume.
  • A common model, so any shop has seen one and parts are everywhere.
  • An insurance quote on the VIN, obtained before the deposit.
  • An inspection by someone who isn't selling you the car.

The part no car can fix

The largest variables in a new driver's first year aren't in the vehicle. Supervised hours in rain and in the dark. A phone rule that's enforced rather than announced. A passenger rule. And a standing agreement that a call for a ride, at any hour, carries no consequences — the cheapest safety equipment available, and the only kind that works when everything else already has gone wrong.

New Jersey runs a graduated driver license program with specific restrictions on hours, passengers and decals for new drivers, and those rules change from time to time. The MVC publishes the current version. Read it there rather than taking our summary of it, because ours would be a summary written by a car dealer.

The inspection that pays for itself. Have someone who isn't selling you the car put it on a lift first. Every car we sell carries its full 168-point inspection, published — and we still tell people to take it to their own mechanic, because the useful second opinion is the one you commissioned.

This is a buying framework, not a safety rating. We have used no crash or injury figures here, because we hold no data of that kind and wouldn't be a credible source for it. Per-model crash test results are published by NHTSA and IIHS; graduated license rules are published by the New Jersey MVC. Insurance costs vary by driver, vehicle and carrier.
