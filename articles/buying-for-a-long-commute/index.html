Buying

Buying for a long commute

At 25,000 miles a year, the purchase price stops being the main event. Everything that gets charged per mile moves to the front, and a few things people shop hardest for turn out not to matter at all.

Drive Thru Deals · September 17, 2026 · 6 min read

Twenty-five thousand miles a year is roughly fifty miles each way, five days a week, with weekends on top. It's a common enough shape in New Jersey — one end of the Turnpike to the other, or a job over a bridge — and it changes the buying problem in a specific way: costs that scale with distance stop being background noise and become the budget.

What follows is ten criteria, ranked. The bars are our weighting, drawn so the shape is visible at a glance. They are a judgment, not a measurement — nobody has weighed these against each other in a lab, and we're not going to pretend otherwise. The arithmetic underneath the top few, though, is arithmetic you can redo yourself with your own numbers.

Fuel cost per mile

Weight: highest

This is the one that dwarfs everything else, and it's the one people casually round off in the showroom. The gap between two cars that seem close on the sticker is not close over a year at this mileage.

Tires and the wear parts behind them

Weight: very high

Tires are consumed by distance, so the replacement calendar you're used to doesn't apply. If a set lasts 45,000 miles, at 25,000 a year you're buying tires about every 21 months instead of every four years.

Which makes tire size a buying criterion, and almost nobody treats it as one. A car on 17-inch wheels and a car on 20-inch wheels can be the same car with a different appearance package and a materially different cost of ownership at your mileage. Price a set for the exact size on the car in front of you before you buy it. It takes two minutes.

Service cadence, and what one visit costs

Weight: very high

Everything on a mileage interval arrives faster. Whatever the manual for your specific car says — and read the manual, not the internet, and not us — divide 25,000 by it. If the number is 7,500 miles, that's three and a third services a year, every year.

So the thing to price isn't the interval, it's the interval multiplied by what a visit costs on that car. Cars with awkward filter locations, specialty fluids, or a transmission service that most independent shops won't touch are more expensive per year at high mileage than their reputation suggests.

The seat, and the driving position

Weight: high

Two hours a day in one chair is a durability question about you, and it's the criterion most often decided by a fifteen-minute test drive around the block. Drive the car for at least as long as one leg of your commute, at the time of day you'd actually drive it, in the traffic you'd actually sit in. Lumbar support, the angle of the seat base, where your right foot rests when you're not on a pedal, and whether the armrest is at a height your elbow agrees with.

This is also the criterion with the shortest feedback loop. You'll know in a week whether you got it right, and there is no fixing it afterward.

Reliability of the things that strand you

Weight: high

Not general reliability — the specific failure modes that stop the car rather than annoy you. A failing window motor at 25,000 miles a year is an inconvenience. A cooling system, a fuel pump or a timing component is a tow truck, a missed day and a rental.

Which argues for a common model with a known service history and a shop near you that has seen a hundred of them, over something unusual that happened to be a good price.

Noise at sustained speed

Weight: moderate

Sustained noise is fatiguing in a way that shows up at the far end of the drive rather than during it. Test it on a highway, with the radio off, at the speed you actually travel. Tire roar is partly the tires and can be changed at the next replacement; wind noise around the mirrors and A-pillar is the car and can't.

Where the car sits on its depreciation curve

Weight: moderate

You are going to add mileage fast, and mileage is one of the things the market prices. The defense is to buy a car that has already taken its steepest drop, so that the miles you add are landing on a flatter part of the curve. A three-year-old car losing value gently is a better host for 75,000 of your miles than a one-year-old car that hasn't finished falling.

Insurance on the specific vehicle

Weight: moderate

It's a fixed annual cost rather than a per-mile one, which is why it sits here and not higher — but it varies enough between two similar cars to be worth ten minutes. Quote the VIN, and tell your agent your real annual mileage. Some carriers ask, and an estimate you invented at the time of the quote is a bad thing to have on a policy.

Driver assistance and cruise

Weight: low to moderate

Adaptive cruise is genuinely pleasant in stop-and-go traffic and genuinely reduces how tired you are at the end of a bad Tuesday. It's also an option package, which means it's priced, and at this mileage the money it costs is competing with three years of the fuel difference at the top of this list. Nice if it's there on the car you'd have bought anyway. A poor reason to move up a trim level.

Size, power, trim, color

Weight: near zero

The things most shopping time gets spent on. At 25,000 miles a year they are almost entirely decoration — with one exception worth naming: a black interior in a car that lives in a parking lot all summer is a real consideration, and the only one in this row that we'd let change a decision.

The arithmetic behind the top of the list

Two cars, 25,000 miles, illustrative figures

Say one car returns 22 mpg in your real-world driving and the other returns 32. Over 25,000 miles that's 1,136 gallons against 781 gallons — a difference of about 355 gallons a year.

We're deliberately not putting a price on a gallon, because that number moves and we'd be inventing it. Multiply 355 by whatever you actually pay, and then multiply that by the number of years you plan to keep the car. For most people the result is larger than the price difference they spent three weekends negotiating.

Both mpg figures are illustrative round numbers chosen to make the shape legible. Use the real-world numbers for the specific cars you're comparing, not the window sticker's, and see the second-car arithmetic for the same method applied to a different question.

The thing that runs backward

High mileage is treated as a defect, and on a commuter car it frequently isn't. Highway miles are the gentlest miles a car can accumulate: the engine reaches temperature and stays there, the transmission holds one gear, the brakes barely work, and the whole drivetrain avoids the thermal cycling that actually wears things out. A 90,000-mile car that did nothing but I-287 can be in better mechanical condition than a 45,000-mile car that spent six years doing four-mile trips in Newark.

You can't tell which you're looking at from the odometer, which is the point. You can tell from service records, from the wear pattern on the brakes and tires, and from what the car looks like underneath. If you're buying for a long commute, the high-mileage highway car is often the value in the market, because everyone else is pricing the number on the dash.

One test worth insisting on. Drive the candidate on the highway at your actual commuting speed, for at least twenty minutes, with the climate control running the way you'd run it. Noise, seat comfort, how the car tracks and whether the transmission hunts at 70 are all things that only appear after the car has settled. A loop around the block tests none of them.
